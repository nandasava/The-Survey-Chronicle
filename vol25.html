A currency is nothing but a shared story: this piece of metal, paper, or code is worth something, because everyone agrees it is. Most of history's currencies broke that story eventually — through war debt, royal greed, or reckless printing. A handful haven't. This volume traces the line from an 8th-century English silver penny to the digital assets people now buy as insurance against the same old mistake.

I.The First Pound: King Offa's Silver Penny

Around 775–800 AD, England was not a country — it was seven rival Anglo-Saxon kingdoms constantly at war. King Offa of Mercia, wanting a reliable way to trade with Europe and pay his soldiers, standardized a silver penny. Gather 240 of them, and they weighed exactly one pound of high-purity "sterling" silver — the literal origin of the name pound sterling.

It wasn't paper money. It was a physical weight of precious metal, and that physical backing is exactly why it held value: nobody had to trust a promise, only a scale.

II.One King, One Currency: Athelstan's Rule

After Viking invasions wiped out most of the rival Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, only Wessex survived, under Alfred the Great. His grandson, King Athelstan, finally unified all of England under one crown in 927 AD — and immediately moved to control the money supply. He decreed a single currency across the entire realm, minted only by the king. Controlling currency became, quite literally, how you controlled a country.

III.Six Dynasties, One Unbroken Pound

After the Norman Conquest of 1066, the English crown passed through a rotating cast of royal families — but every single one kept the pound sterling, because global trust in it was too valuable to discard.

Today's King Charles III, of the House of Windsor, simply has his face stamped on coins that trace their lineage back to a Mercian warlord's silver pennies — over 1,200 years of continuous use.

IV.Henry VIII, "Old Coppernose," and the Great Debasement

Not every chapter of the pound's history was honest. Between 1544 and 1551, Henry VIII — drained by foreign wars and lavish spending — ordered the Royal Mint to secretly dilute England's silver coins with cheap copper, while stamping them with the same face value. By the end of the scheme, coins that had been 92.5% pure silver were cut down to just 33.3% silver.

The fraud became visible in an almost poetic way: the coin's highest-relief point was Henry's nose, so as coins wore down through daily use, the thin silver coating rubbed off there first, exposing the copper underneath. The public nicknamed their king "Old Coppernose."

The economic fallout

What followed is now a textbook case of Gresham's Law — "bad money drives out good." People hoarded the older, pure-silver coins and smuggled them abroad, while merchants demanded three or four of the new debased coins for goods that used to cost one. Prices spiked, and the poor absorbed the worst of it. It took Henry's daughter, Elizabeth I, years to recall the corrupted coinage and restore the sterling standard.

"The coin's nose gave away the scam. A king can dilute silver, but he can't dilute friction."

V.The Swiss Franc: 176 Years Without a Collapse

Switzerland unified its chaotic patchwork of over 800 regional coins into a single national currency with the Federal Coinage Act of 1850, initially pegged 1:1 with the French franc. The Swiss National Bank began issuing standardized paper notes in 1907, and the franc kept a formal gold backing until that link was finally cut in 2000.

The franc's oldest surviving design — the 10-rappen coin, first minted in 1879 — is still legal tender today and holds the Guinness World Record for the oldest original coin design in active circulation. That unbroken 176-year track record, without hyperinflation or default, is exactly why global investors reach for the Swiss franc whenever confidence in bigger currencies wobbles.

The ZiG replaced the rapidly inflating Zimbabwean dollar, and was explicitly designed to be backed by a physical basket of gold, diamonds, and foreign reserves — a modern echo of the same trust problem Offa's silver penny solved 1,250 years earlier.

VII.What 1,200 Years Teaches About Trust

Every durable currency in this history shares one trait: a track record of not being secretly diluted, defaulted on, or hyperinflated. Every failed one shares the opposite — a ruler or government quietly chipping away at what a unit of currency was actually worth, hoping nobody would notice until the debt was paid.

Modern debates about de-dollarization, gold-backed reserves, and Bitcoin's fixed supply cap are the same conversation Anglo-Saxon merchants were having with Offa's silver pennies — just with faster technology and a much larger audience.

VIII.Frequently Asked Questions

What is the oldest currency still in use?

The British pound sterling, dating back to around 775–800 AD during the Anglo-Saxon period, is widely considered the world's oldest currency still in continuous use.

What is the youngest currency in the world?

The Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG), launched on April 5, 2024 to replace the hyperinflated Zimbabwean dollar, is currently the world's youngest currency.

Why did Henry VIII put copper in silver coins?

Henry VIII was funding expensive wars and needed more currency than his silver reserves allowed. He ordered coins diluted with copper while keeping their stamped face value the same, a scheme known as the Great Debasement (1544–1551).

Why is the Swiss franc considered a safe-haven currency?

The Swiss franc has a 176-year track record without hyperinflation or default, historically backed by gold, which makes it a preferred store of value during global financial uncertainty.

How old is the Swiss franc?

The modern Swiss franc was established in 1850 under the Federal Coinage Act, making it 176 years old as of 2026.
