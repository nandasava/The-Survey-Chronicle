Editorial introduction

A History Before the Kings

This volume begins before there was an England, before there was a United Kingdom, before the word Britain meant a nation, and long before written chronicles could tell us who ruled. It follows the evidence backward into landscapes occupied by hunter-gatherers, early farmers, metalworkers, warriors, merchants and communities that built monuments whose meanings still invite debate. It then follows Britain into the Roman world, where conquest created new towns, roads, administrative structures, military frontiers and connections to an empire stretching far beyond the island.

The dates in this volume are deliberately handled with care. Prehistory is not a simple list of kings because there are no surviving royal lists for most of the period. Archaeologists reconstruct chronology from material remains, environmental evidence and scientific dating. Roman Britain offers more written evidence, but Roman authors had political purposes and cultural assumptions. Where evidence is debated, this volume identifies uncertainty instead of presenting interpretation as established fact.

The chronological endpoint is around AD 410, when Roman administrative rule ceased to operate in Britain as it had before. That transition leads directly into Volume 29. The central argument of the volume is not that Britain followed one straight path from primitive society to civilisation. Rather, its history is a succession of adaptations, migrations, technological changes, political experiments and cultural exchanges.

Chapter 01

The Island Before the Island

Britain did not begin as an island nation. The landscapes that later generations would call Britain were repeatedly reshaped by ice, rivers, sea level, tectonic forces and changing climates. Human history here therefore begins with geography before it becomes a story about kings, tribes or states. The earliest communities encountered a moving world whose coastlines could disappear beneath water or emerge from it again. Archaeology at places such as Happisburgh, Boxgrove and other Palaeolithic sites demonstrates that human occupation of these lands reaches deep into the Ice Age. English Heritage places the earliest known human arrival in what is now England at roughly 900,000 years ago, while the exact patterns of occupation varied with climate and species.

The most important lesson of deep British prehistory is that there was never one continuous, unchanging population living according to one culture. Different hominins entered, left, returned or disappeared as ecological conditions changed. Neanderthals occupied parts of Britain during several periods, while later modern humans left evidence in caves, camps and symbolic objects. During colder phases Britain could be connected to continental Europe by land, while warmer periods transformed the same territory into a peninsula and eventually an island. Rising sea levels around the end of the last Ice Age severed the land connection, producing a coastline closer to the one familiar today.

For a history of the United Kingdom, this deep beginning matters because later ideas of England, Scotland, Wales and Britain were built on landscapes already inhabited, named and used for many thousands of years. Before written history, people were already choosing seasonal routes, exploiting rivers, organising food production, building monuments and maintaining long-distance contacts. The result is a historical foundation much older than any dynasty covered in later volumes of The Survey Chronicle.

Chapter 02

Ice Age Britain and the Earliest People

During the Palaeolithic, Britain was repeatedly affected by glacial cycles. Ice sheets advanced and retreated, tundra and open grassland replaced forests, and animals such as mammoth, rhinoceros and reindeer occupied environments very different from modern Britain. Human groups followed these ecological opportunities. Their material traces are often small: flint tools, butchered animal bones, hearths and rare human remains. Yet those modest traces reveal sophisticated survival strategies in environments that could be extraordinarily demanding.

Sites such as Boxgrove have transformed understanding of early humans in Britain. Evidence from the site includes stone tools and butchered animal remains associated with Homo heidelbergensis. Much later, Neanderthals occupied parts of the country, adapting to cold environments and making distinctive stone tools. At Creswell Crags, cave art dating to around 13,000 years ago provides evidence not simply of survival but of symbolic expression. The distance between a stone handaxe and later monumental architecture is therefore not a story of people suddenly becoming intelligent; it is a story of changing technologies, communities, environments and archaeological visibility.

The Palaeolithic world cannot be reconstructed through kings or written laws because none survive from it. Archaeologists instead build arguments from deposits, landscape changes, animal remains, tools and microscopic traces. That evidence also contains uncertainty. Dates are often ranges rather than single moments, and the absence of an artefact does not prove that a behaviour never happened. A serious history of prehistoric Britain therefore distinguishes between what the evidence demonstrates, what it strongly suggests and what remains unknown.

Chapter 03

Mesolithic Britain: Hunters, Fishers and Landscapes

After the last Ice Age, temperatures rose and environments changed rapidly. Britain became increasingly forested, wetlands expanded, and mobile communities exploited a broad range of plants and animals. The Mesolithic is often described as a hunter-gatherer period, but that label can obscure the diversity of activities involved. People hunted red deer and wild cattle, collected shellfish, gathered plants, fished, trapped animals and repeatedly returned to favoured locations. Seasonal mobility did not mean that communities lacked attachment to particular landscapes.

Archaeological discoveries have shown that some Mesolithic communities constructed substantial structures. At Star Carr in Yorkshire, for example, evidence points to repeated occupation, woodworking and ritual activity in a wetland environment. The preservation of organic material in waterlogged contexts has allowed archaeologists to recover objects that would normally disappear, including worked wood and animal remains. Such sites make it possible to examine social behaviour rather than relying solely on surviving stone artefacts.

The final Mesolithic centuries also witnessed the dramatic separation of Britain from continental Europe. As glaciers melted, sea levels rose and the land bridge was inundated. By about 6500 BC Britain had become an island. This geographic fact influenced later trade, migration and cultural transmission without isolating Britain completely. Maritime movement remained possible, and the archaeological record shows continuing connections across the Irish Sea and English Channel. Islandhood created a distinctive setting, not a sealed world.

Chapter 04

The Arrival of Farming

The transition to farming was one of the most consequential changes in British prehistory. Neolithic communities cultivated crops, kept domesticated animals and reorganised relationships with land. Yet farming did not arrive as a single event or instantly replace hunting and gathering. Archaeologists increasingly emphasise a process involving movement of people, exchange of animals and materials, adoption of new practices and interaction between newcomers and existing communities.

The introduction of agriculture changed the rhythm of life. Fields had to be cultivated, herds managed and food stored. Permanent or semi-permanent settlements became increasingly important. Pottery, polished stone tools and new forms of architecture appeared. Forest clearance altered the landscape, while domesticated cattle, sheep, goats and pigs created new forms of wealth and labour. Communities also invested remarkable effort in monuments, indicating that economic production and ceremonial life were closely connected.

Long-distance exchange reveals how connected Neolithic Britain could be. Stone suitable for axes travelled across regions, and styles of pottery and monuments show interaction over substantial distances. English Heritage notes that Atlantic networks carried ideas about megalithic tombs between Brittany, western Britain, Wales, Scotland and Ireland. The Neolithic landscape was therefore not a collection of isolated villages; it was a network of communities capable of moving people, objects, stories and practices.

Chapter 05

Stonehenge and the Age of Monuments

Stonehenge is the most famous prehistoric monument in Britain, but its importance becomes clearer when placed beside the wider landscape of monuments surrounding Salisbury Plain and elsewhere. Long barrows, causewayed enclosures, henges, timber structures, avenues and stone circles demonstrate that prehistoric communities invested labour in places that had functions extending beyond ordinary domestic settlement. The construction process itself would have required coordination, food, tools, transport, skilled knowledge and social organisation.

The stones at Stonehenge were not all local. Bluestones came from Wales, while larger sarsen stones were obtained from sources farther afield. Their movement suggests extensive networks and a capacity to organise difficult transport. The monument also changed through time; Stonehenge was not a single construction project completed once. Successive communities modified, rebuilt and reused the site. Understanding that sequence is more informative than treating Stonehenge as the product of one mysterious people.

The purpose of prehistoric monuments cannot be reduced to a single modern category such as temple, cemetery or calendar. Some sites combined ceremonial, mortuary and social functions. Burial practices, feasting deposits, animal remains and human remains all contribute to interpretation. What is clear is that communities repeatedly returned to monumental places and invested them with meanings that connected ancestry, landscape, memory and collective identity.

Chapter 06

Bronze Age Britain and the Metal Revolution

The Bronze Age introduced new technologies, new exchange networks and new forms of social differentiation. Copper and tin had to be obtained and combined to make bronze, linking British communities to wider European networks. Metal tools and weapons were more than practical objects: they could signal status, wealth and membership in networks of power. Hoards found by archaeologists sometimes contain objects that were never recovered by their owners, raising questions about ritual deposition, emergency concealment and exchange.

The Beaker phenomenon of the later third millennium BC is particularly important. Distinctive pottery, burial customs and associated artefacts appeared across large parts of Europe, including Britain. Ancient DNA research has transformed the debate by demonstrating major population replacement in Britain during this period. The evidence suggests that the arrival of Beaker-associated communities was associated with a substantial demographic transformation rather than merely the adoption of a new fashion.

During the Middle and Late Bronze Age, settlement patterns became more visibly organised. Roundhouses, fields, trackways and boundaries can survive archaeologically even where no written record exists. Landscapes were increasingly divided and managed. Wealth was still unevenly distributed, and some burials contain exceptional objects. Bronze Age Britain therefore provides an early example of a society in which agriculture, craft production, long-distance exchange and social status interacted to produce complex communities.

Chapter 07

Iron Age Britain: Tribes, Hillforts and Power

The Iron Age is often presented as the age of Celtic tribes and hillforts, but neither term should be treated as a simple description of one unified culture. Britain contained many communities with different languages, traditions and political structures. Iron-working became widespread, while agriculture intensified. Some regions saw substantial settlements and defensive earthworks; others remained more dispersed. The result was a varied political landscape rather than a single British kingdom.

Hillforts are among the most visible monuments of Iron Age Britain. Their functions varied. Some were defensive, some were elite centres, some organised agricultural landscapes and some may have served ceremonial or gathering functions. Maiden Castle in Dorset is one of the best-known examples. Large ramparts required substantial collective labour, demonstrating that communities could mobilise people on a scale larger than the household.

Late Iron Age society also became increasingly connected to the Roman world. Coins entered circulation, imported goods reached tribal elites and continental political developments became relevant to British leaders. The emergence of client kingdoms and minting traditions shows that economic and political change was already under way before the Roman conquest. This matters because Rome did not transform an untouched island; it entered a complex world with its own hierarchies and international contacts.

Chapter 08

Druids, Religion and Sacred Places

Religion in prehistoric and Iron Age Britain is difficult to reconstruct because the people themselves left no surviving theological texts. Roman authors wrote about Druids and religious practices, but those accounts were produced by outsiders with their own political motives and cultural assumptions. Archaeology provides another line of evidence through sanctuaries, deposits, burials, unusual objects and places where animals or weapons were deliberately deposited.

Sacred activity appears to have involved water, wetlands, caves, springs, groves and purpose-built enclosures. Objects deposited in unusual contexts may represent offerings, while human remains can indicate complex ritual treatment. The famous association between Druids and oak groves comes primarily from classical literary traditions. Modern historians must therefore separate evidence for Iron Age ritual specialists from later romantic images of white-robed Druids dominating every aspect of Celtic society.

Roman conquest changed the conditions under which indigenous religious practices operated. Some cults continued, others adapted, and new Roman and eastern religions appeared. The transformation demonstrates how religious history can be a record of negotiation rather than simple replacement. In Britain, local gods could be paired with Roman deities, while temples could adopt architectural forms associated with the empire without abandoning local identities.

Chapter 09

Julius Caesar Looks Across the Channel

In 55 and 54 BC Julius Caesar crossed the Channel during his campaigns in Gaul. These expeditions were not the permanent Roman conquest of Britain, but they mattered because Caesar left literary descriptions that became some of the earliest surviving written accounts of the island. He described geography, social organisation, warfare and political relationships, but his account was also a piece of military propaganda intended for readers in Rome.

Caesar encountered resistance and practical difficulties. Weather interfered with operations, British forces used chariots, and the Roman army lacked the infrastructure required for a long occupation. Hostages and diplomatic agreements offered temporary political outcomes without creating a Roman province. After Caesar's departure, trade and diplomatic links between Britain and Rome continued, and Roman goods became more visible in southern Britain.

The significance of Caesar's invasions therefore lies partly in what they did not accomplish. They did not make Britain Roman. Instead, they opened a century of increasing interaction. British rulers participated in continental politics, imported Roman prestige goods and sometimes sought Roman support. By the time Claudius invaded nearly a century later, the strategic and political relationship between Britain and Rome was substantially different from the one Caesar had encountered.

Chapter 10

Why Claudius Invaded Britain

The Roman invasion under Emperor Claudius in AD 43 marked the beginning of nearly four centuries of Roman administration. The invasion had political value for Claudius, who was a relatively new emperor seeking military prestige, but Britain also had strategic and economic importance. Roman sources suggest that disputes among British rulers and the position of anti-Roman or pro-Roman leaders influenced the decision to intervene.

The first campaign involved a substantial military force, including four legions, auxiliaries and supporting units. Roman forces landed in the south-east and advanced against British resistance. Richborough became an important gateway, while campaigns pushed toward major tribal centres. Claudius himself travelled to Britain and received the symbolic prestige of victory. The conquest, however, was far from complete in AD 43.

Over the following decades Rome expanded its control through campaigns, diplomacy, client kingships and administrative reorganisation. England and Wales were brought under much firmer control than the far north. The process was uneven because geography and political resistance varied. Roman Britain was therefore a conquest that unfolded over generations, not a single battle that instantly created a province.

Chapter 11

The Conquest of Wales and the North

Wales posed sustained resistance to Roman expansion. Campaigns against the Silures, Ordovices and other communities required repeated military operations. The region's mountainous terrain made movement difficult and encouraged the Romans to rely on forts, roads and military zones. Anglesey was also strategically important because Roman writers associated it with Druids and resistance.

In northern Britain, the Roman army confronted different strategic problems. Control required long supply lines and a permanent military presence. Agricola's campaigns in the late first century pushed northward, and the Battle of Mons Graupius is described by Tacitus, although details of the battle remain debated. Roman forces reached far into Scotland but did not maintain permanent control of all territories north of the later frontier.

These frontier wars reveal an important characteristic of Roman Britain: imperial power had limits. Rome could build roads and forts, defeat field armies and install local administrations, yet frontier communities retained their own interests. The eventual line of Hadrian's Wall was not simply an arbitrary boundary. It reflected a decision about where the empire could most effectively combine military security, administration and economic control.

Chapter 12

Boudica and the Great Revolt of AD 60–61

Boudica's revolt is one of the most famous episodes in British history. She was queen of the Iceni, a client kingdom in eastern Britain. According to Tacitus, after the death of her husband Prasutagus, Roman treatment of the kingdom and its ruling family contributed to conflict. Tacitus describes the flogging of Boudica and the abuse of her daughters, while Roman financial demands and confiscations intensified resentment. The exact details should be read cautiously because the surviving accounts were written by Roman authors decades later.

The revolt rapidly expanded. Camulodunum was attacked and destroyed, followed by Londinium and Verulamium. Roman forces were initially stretched because Suetonius Paulinus was campaigning in Wales. Eventually he gathered a smaller but disciplined force and confronted the rebels at an unidentified location. Tacitus describes a decisive Roman victory followed by severe casualties among the rebel army.

Boudica's revolt demonstrates both the violence of conquest and the political complexity of client kingdoms. It also shows why provincial administration depended on cooperation as well as military force. After the revolt, Roman policy in the Iceni region appears to have been adjusted. The story survives not only as an ancient episode but as a later symbol repeatedly reinterpreted in Victorian Britain and modern popular culture.

Chapter 13

Londinium: The Making of Roman London

London began as a Roman settlement around the tidal Thames and developed into the most important urban centre in Roman Britain. Its success depended on geography. The river offered transport into the interior, while the location connected routes from Kent and the Channel to communities farther north and west. Archaeological excavations have revealed warehouses, streets, buildings, drainage systems, temples, markets and domestic spaces.

The city was destroyed during Boudica's revolt but rebuilt. Over time it acquired increasingly monumental architecture, including a forum and basilica. London was not simply a military headquarters; it was a commercial and administrative centre. Merchants moved goods through the port, while officials collected taxes and managed provincial government.

The history of Londinium also reveals the uneven character of Romanisation. Urban life introduced new architectural forms, money and administrative practices, but indigenous traditions continued. People entering the city came from many regions. The result was a cosmopolitan environment in which Roman imperial culture interacted with local British communities and wider Mediterranean influences.

Chapter 14

Roads, Towns and the Roman Transformation of the Landscape

Roman rule left a durable physical imprint on Britain. Roads connected military forts, towns, ports and agricultural regions. Surveyors planned routes, engineers built surfaces and drainage, and the army often played a major role in early construction. Some modern roads follow Roman alignments, while others survive as field boundaries or earthworks.

Roman towns transformed settlement patterns. Places such as Verulamium, Calleva Atrebatum, Wroxeter and Bath developed forums, public buildings, workshops and housing. Urbanisation was never universal: many people continued to live in rural communities. Yet towns became centres where taxation, markets, administration, craft production and social display converged.

The countryside changed as estates, farms and villas developed. Villas could range from modest rural residences to highly decorated complexes with mosaic floors, bath suites and agricultural infrastructure. Roman Britain's landscape was therefore neither entirely urban nor entirely rural. It was a connected economic system in which roads moved agricultural products, people, soldiers and information between settlements.

Chapter 15

Roman Britain and the Economy

The Roman economy in Britain relied on taxation, agriculture, military expenditure, local production and long-distance trade. Grain and other foodstuffs supported the army and towns. Lead from southwest Britain, metals from Wales and other mineral resources entered imperial networks. Coins circulated as part of taxation and commerce, though the degree to which every transaction used coinage varied.

Military bases generated enormous demand. Soldiers required food, clothing, animals, weapons, building materials and services. Civilian communities responded by supplying markets. Pottery kilns, metalworking areas, textile production and agricultural estates are all visible in the archaeological record. Ports connected Britain to Gaul and beyond, allowing imported wine, olive oil, pottery and luxury goods to reach provincial consumers.

Economic integration also produced inequality. Wealthy landowners could display status through villas and imported objects, while many agricultural workers lived much more modestly. Enslaved people were part of the Roman economy, although the documentary and archaeological record makes their individual experiences difficult to reconstruct. A history of Roman Britain therefore needs to account for both prosperity and exploitation.

Chapter 16

The Roman Army in Britain

The Roman army was the central institution of Roman power in Britain. Legions formed the core of the military force, supported by auxiliary units recruited from across the empire. Soldiers came from many regions and brought languages, customs and religious traditions with them. Their presence created communities around forts and military roads.

Forts varied from temporary campaign bases to large permanent installations. They included barracks, granaries, workshops, headquarters, hospitals and commanding officers' residences. Vindolanda's writing tablets provide unusually vivid glimpses into military administration and everyday correspondence. Such evidence shows soldiers writing about supplies, leave, clothing and personal relationships rather than only about battles.

Military service could also create opportunities. Auxiliary soldiers could eventually receive Roman citizenship after completing service, linking provincial populations to the empire's legal structure. Yet life at the frontier remained demanding. Cold weather, isolation, disease, discipline and the possibility of violence all shaped soldiers' experiences. The army was therefore simultaneously an instrument of conquest, a labour force, an employer and a channel of cultural exchange.

Chapter 17

Hadrian's Wall and the Northern Frontier

In AD 122, the emperor Hadrian visited Britain and ordered construction of a frontier system that became known as Hadrian's Wall. English Heritage places the project in the context of Roman frontier policy and describes the wall as one of the most famous monuments of Roman Britain. The structure included stone and turf sections, forts, milecastles, turrets, roads and associated civilian settlements.

The wall was not simply an impenetrable barrier. Gates regulated movement, military units controlled crossing points and roads connected the frontier to the wider province. The frontier zone was a place of trade, recruitment and interaction as well as confrontation. People living near the boundary had to negotiate with Roman authorities and soldiers.

The wall also changed meaning over time. Its strategic role shifted as emperors altered frontier policies. The Antonine Wall farther north briefly extended Roman control in the mid-second century, but the Romans later returned to Hadrian's line. The shifting frontier demonstrates that Roman Britain was part of an empire whose borders were negotiated through military capacity, political decisions and economic realities.

Chapter 18

Scotland and the Limits of Roman Rule

Roman attempts to control what is now Scotland were episodic. Agricola campaigned north in the late first century, and later emperors changed the frontier. The Antonine Wall was built during the reign of Antoninus Pius but was occupied for a relatively short period. Its abandonment in favour of Hadrian's Wall illustrated the challenges of sustaining a northern frontier.

Roman accounts use names such as Caledonians and Picts for northern peoples, but modern historians must avoid assuming that these labels describe stable nations in the modern sense. Communities north of the frontier included groups with changing alliances and local identities. Their political organisation was different from the administrative structures of the Roman province.

Roman influence nevertheless reached beyond the formal frontier through trade, diplomacy and military encounters. Objects made in the Roman world have been found far north of Hadrian's Wall. Frontier history was consequently not a simple line between Roman and non-Roman worlds; it was a zone of interaction where identities, goods and power crossed boundaries.

Chapter 19

Wales, Britain and Cultural Survival

Roman occupation transformed Wales but did not erase indigenous communities. Forts and roads established imperial control, yet many settlements continued to use local traditions. The resistance associated with the Silures shows that conquest was contested. Later military installations reveal how strategically important the region remained.

Religious life offers another example of continuity and change. Indigenous deities remained important, sometimes appearing in inscriptions under Romanised names. Temples could follow architectural patterns associated with the empire while preserving local religious traditions. The same process can be seen in language and personal names, where Latin and indigenous forms existed side by side.

The historical importance of Wales in Roman Britain also lies in what happened after Roman administration weakened. Some Roman military sites and settlements remained in use into the post-Roman period. The later history of Britain cannot therefore be divided into a neat Roman chapter followed by an instant Anglo-Saxon replacement. Continuity, adaptation and regional variation are essential parts of the story.

Chapter 20

Baths, Water and the Roman Way of Life

Roman baths are among the best-known features of provincial Roman culture. Bath complexes served purposes that extended beyond washing. They were places for exercise, socialising, business, relaxation and sometimes religious activity. The surviving remains at Bath show how architectural ambition and natural resources could combine to create a major provincial centre.

Bathing technology depended on engineering. Hypocaust systems moved hot air beneath floors, while furnaces heated rooms and water. Aqueducts and drainage systems supplied and removed water. Such facilities required labour, fuel and maintenance, which means that the bathhouse was also part of a wider economic and administrative system.

Yet the Roman way of life was never identical everywhere. People adapted Roman customs to local conditions. Not every household had access to a private bath or imported luxury goods. Social class strongly shaped experience. Archaeology therefore allows historians to explore both the prestige of Roman culture and the practical diversity of life across the province.

Chapter 21

Homes, Villas and Ordinary People

Roman Britain contained an enormous range of homes. Some people lived in roundhouses that retained architectural traditions with deep pre-Roman roots. Others lived in timber-framed urban houses, modest rural buildings or elaborate villas. The diversity of housing reflects the diversity of Britain's population and social classes.

Villas often included mosaics, heated rooms and agricultural facilities. Their owners may have been local elites who adopted Roman styles to display status and participate in provincial society. But the presence of a villa does not mean that everyone in the surrounding countryside lived comfortably. Agricultural labour, tenancy, dependency and slavery formed part of the rural economy.

Children, women, workers and craftspeople remain less visible in written sources than elite men, but archaeology can recover traces of their lives. Objects found in houses, cemeteries and workshops reveal cooking practices, clothing, occupations and personal adornment. The history of Roman Britain becomes richer when these everyday experiences are treated as central rather than peripheral.

Chapter 22

Food, Clothing and Daily Life

Food provides one of the clearest ways to see the interaction of local traditions and Roman influence. Grain remained fundamental, while meat, fish, vegetables, fruit and imported products varied according to region and social status. Olive oil and wine reached Britain through trade, while locally produced beer continued to be important.

Clothing also communicated identity. Roman-style garments existed alongside local forms, and material remains such as brooches, shoes and textile tools reveal changing fashions. Personal objects could signal occupation, status, gender or regional affiliation. The spread of Roman goods did not necessarily mean that people abandoned previous identities; objects could be incorporated into existing social practices.

Domestic life included cooking, childcare, craft production and household management. The evidence is fragmentary, but enough survives to show that Roman Britain was inhabited by people making practical decisions every day. Historical change happened through these small routines as much as through imperial decrees or military campaigns.

Chapter 23

Women and Family in Roman Britain

Women in Roman Britain lived within legal and social structures influenced by Roman law but shaped by local traditions. Elite women could own property, manage households and participate in religious life. Inscriptions and funerary monuments reveal names, family connections and occupations, although the surviving evidence disproportionately represents wealthier people.

Marriage could connect families and consolidate property or political relationships. Inscriptions show both Roman and indigenous naming traditions. Women also appear as business owners, patrons, religious figures and workers, though the exact distribution of occupations varies by region and social class.

Children were another important part of family life. Infant burials, toys and household objects demonstrate that childhood was recognised through material culture. Mortality remained high by modern standards, and family histories could be shaped by repeated bereavement. Studying women and children helps move beyond a narrative focused exclusively on emperors, governors and soldiers.

Chapter 24

Slavery, Status and Inequality

The Roman Empire was a hierarchical society, and Britain participated in systems of slavery and dependency. Enslaved people could work in households, agriculture, workshops and other settings. Archaeology cannot always identify enslaved individuals directly, but legal evidence and the broader structure of Roman society make their presence certain.

Status was visible through housing, clothing, burial practices and access to public institutions. Senators and wealthy provincial elites occupied very different worlds from tenant farmers, labourers and enslaved people. Citizenship also mattered. Auxiliary military service, imperial grants and family status could change a person's legal position.

Roman Britain should therefore not be described simply as a place where everyone became equally Roman. Romanisation operated unevenly, and its benefits were distributed through existing and newly created hierarchies. The province was integrated into an empire whose political and economic order depended on unequal access to power.

Chapter 25

Religion: Native Gods, Roman Gods and New Faiths

Roman Britain contained a remarkable mixture of religious traditions. Local deities continued to be worshipped, while Roman gods were introduced and sometimes combined with indigenous ones. Inscriptions at temples and shrines provide evidence of this blending. The term syncretism can be useful, but it should not imply that people simply merged religions mechanically. Religious practice was local and personal as well as political.

Mystery religions and eastern cults also reached Britain, especially through military and commercial networks. Mithras is the most famous example, with remains of Mithraea found in several locations. Such cults demonstrate that Roman Britain participated in the wider religious diversity of the empire.

Christianity eventually became a major force, but its development was gradual. There is evidence of Christian communities during the later Roman period, and imperial policy changed dramatically after Constantine. Christianity did not instantly erase older practices. Religious transformation unfolded over generations and differed from region to region.

Chapter 26

The Third-Century Crisis Reaches Britain

The third century brought repeated political and military crises to the Roman Empire. Britain was affected by civil wars, frontier raids, economic disruption and changes in imperial administration. The provincial response included fortification and changes to military organisation. Town walls became more prominent, signalling concern about security.

Britain also experienced breakaway political regimes. The Gallic Empire of the 260s and 270s included Britain, while Carausius and Allectus controlled Britain during the 280s and 290s. Their governments demonstrate that the province could become the centre of alternative imperial projects rather than simply a passive recipient of decisions from Rome.

Constantius Chlorus restored imperial authority in AD 296, and Britain later became associated with Constantine the Great, who was proclaimed emperor at York in AD 306. The event shows that Britain remained politically important even as the empire changed. The province was integrated into imperial power struggles as much as it was affected by them.

Chapter 27

Constantine and York: Britain at the Centre of Empire

In AD 306, Constantine was proclaimed emperor at York after the death of his father, Constantius Chlorus. Constantine's subsequent career transformed the Roman world. His association with Christianity and his victories over rivals ultimately established a new imperial order. The later fourth century brought relative stability at some times and renewed pressures at others. Administrative structures became more elaborate, while Christianity gained public visibility. Yet the underlying problems facing the Western Roman Empire became increasingly severe. Chapter 28

The Saxon Shore and Coastal Defence

Late Roman Britain developed a system of coastal forts often associated with the term Saxon Shore. Sites such as Richborough, Reculver, Portchester and others formed part of a defensive network along eastern and southern coasts. The exact purpose of the system remains debated: it may have protected against raiders, secured ports, supported military logistics or performed several functions at once.

Coastal security mattered because Britain was exposed to maritime threats as political conditions changed across the North Sea. Raiding groups could move quickly, and the Roman state needed to protect commercial routes and strategic installations. The forts also formed communities involving soldiers, families, traders and craftspeople.

The phrase Saxon Shore should not be interpreted as proof that Saxons occupied every fort or that Roman officials understood the coastline as a simple ethnic boundary. Archaeology reveals mixed populations and varied patterns of use. Late Roman Britain was a maritime frontier connected to the wider North Sea world.

Chapter 29

The Fourth Century: Christianity, Cities and Change

Towns remained active, villas were renovated in some regions and long-distance trade continued. Chapter 30

The End of Roman Rule: AD 410

The end of Roman administration in Britain was a process rather than a single theatrical event. In AD 410 the emperor Honorius is traditionally associated with a response telling the communities of Britain to look to their own defence. Historians debate exactly how the message was understood and how much Roman authority had already weakened. What is clear is that imperial administration ceased to function in Britain as it had before.

The withdrawal reflected wider pressures on the Western Roman Empire. Britain required troops and administrative resources at the same time as the empire faced conflicts elsewhere. Some military units were removed, local authorities took greater responsibility and urban systems changed. Roman culture did not disappear overnight. Roads, forts, buildings, Christianity and Roman laws continued to influence communities for generations.

The significance of AD 410 is therefore not that Roman Britain suddenly became medieval. It marks the end of one political structure and the beginning of a long period of transformation. Chapter 31

The People of Roman Britain

No single population called itself simply 'Roman Britain'. The province contained indigenous Britons, Roman citizens, soldiers from across the empire, merchants, immigrants, administrators, enslaved people and mixed families. Latin inscriptions sometimes reveal individual origins, while archaeology provides evidence of mobility through pottery, clothing, religious objects and military equipment.

The army was particularly international. Units recruited in continental Europe, North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean could serve in Britain. Some soldiers stayed after service and formed families locally. Their descendants contributed to the multicultural character of provincial life.

This diversity complicates the concept of Romanisation. Becoming Roman could mean acquiring citizenship, participating in urban institutions, adopting Latin, using Roman goods or identifying with imperial political structures. Different people could adopt some aspects without adopting all. Provincial identity was therefore layered, combining local traditions with imperial belonging.

Chapter 32

Chapter 38

What Survived Rome?

When Roman administration disappeared, many aspects of Roman society changed or contracted. Some towns lost population, some villas were abandoned or transformed, and monetary circulation declined. Yet Roman roads, walls, Christian communities, place names and material traditions survived. The transition was selective rather than total.

Some forts continued to be occupied. Communities adapted existing buildings rather than abandoning every Roman site. In the west, post-Roman kingdoms emerged from landscapes shaped by centuries of imperial rule. In the east, new migrants established communities whose cultural traditions were different. The result was a patchwork rather than a clean break.

The legacy of Roman Britain continued to influence medieval and modern historical imagination. Medieval writers remembered Rome as a lost age of cities and roads, while later antiquarians excavated and interpreted Roman remains. Modern archaeology has expanded the story again, revealing ordinary households and regional communities alongside imperial institutions.

Chapter 40

The Historical Method: What We Know and What We Cannot Know

Early British history requires caution. Prehistory is reconstructed primarily from archaeology and environmental science, while Roman Britain combines archaeological evidence with written sources from authors who had their own perspectives. No source is complete. Some periods are richly preserved because particular materials survive, while others remain obscure.

Dates can also change as methods improve. Radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, ancient DNA, isotope analysis, aerial archaeology and geophysical survey have transformed interpretations of prehistoric and Roman Britain. Discoveries can overturn long-established narratives, as happened with debates over migration during the Beaker period.

A long-form history should therefore preserve uncertainty rather than hide it. When evidence is debated, the responsible approach is to explain the competing interpretations and the evidence behind them. The aim is not to make every question sound settled but to show how historians and archaeologists build knowledge from incomplete remains.

Research appendix

Historical Method, Debates and Deeper Explanations

How archaeologists date prehistoric Britain

Archaeologists combine stratigraphy, typology, radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology and environmental evidence. No single method is perfect. Stratigraphy shows which deposits came before others, while radiocarbon provides probability ranges for organic material. When several methods converge, confidence increases. The importance of these techniques is easy to underestimate: without them, a monument such as Stonehenge could be described visually but not placed securely within a long sequence of rebuilding and reuse. Dating is therefore not a decorative footnote. It is the framework that turns scattered objects into a historical chronology.

Ancient DNA and migration

Genetic evidence has changed the interpretation of prehistoric Britain, especially for the Bronze Age. Ancient DNA can reveal patterns of relatedness and population movement that are difficult to see from artefacts alone. It does not, however, give a complete biography of a person or automatically identify a language or political identity. Genetic ancestry, cultural identity and political affiliation are related but not identical questions. The strongest interpretations combine DNA with archaeology, chronology, material culture and environmental evidence.

Why ‘Celtic Britain’ is complicated

The word Celtic is useful in some contexts but can become misleading when it is used to describe one uniform prehistoric British people. Classical writers used different names for different communities. Archaeology shows regional variation. Languages that modern scholars call Celtic existed in parts of western Europe, but the exact distribution and timing of particular languages remain subjects of scholarly debate. A careful history therefore uses Celtic as a linguistic or cultural descriptor when appropriate rather than assuming a single Celtic nation existed across Britain before Rome.

The limits of Roman written sources

Tacitus, Caesar and other classical writers are indispensable, but they were not neutral reporters. Caesar wrote to explain and justify his military campaigns. Tacitus wrote about Roman emperors and governors with strong moral and political themes. Their descriptions of British peoples reflect Roman assumptions about outsiders. Archaeology can confirm, challenge or complicate those narratives. The most persuasive histories treat ancient literature as evidence with a context, not as a transparent recording of events.

What ‘Romanisation’ means

Romanisation once implied that conquered peoples gradually became Roman by adopting Roman culture. Modern scholarship often treats the process as more complicated. Local communities selected, adapted and sometimes resisted imperial practices. A bathhouse, coin or Latin inscription can indicate engagement with Roman systems, but it does not prove that a person abandoned indigenous identity. Roman Britain was a negotiated cultural environment in which local and imperial forms existed together.

Why the Roman conquest took decades

Rome's invasion in AD 43 established a bridgehead, not complete control. Military conquest required campaigns, forts, roads, negotiations and administrative institutions. Wales and northern Britain posed different geographical problems from southeastern England. Some communities resisted for years, while others accepted arrangements with Rome. The decades-long process explains why the archaeology of early Roman Britain contains both military camps and rapidly developing towns.

How Roman roads changed Britain

Roads lowered the cost and time of moving troops, goods and information. They were also visible statements of imperial authority. Surveying a route across difficult terrain required technical skill, while maintaining it required administration and labour. Modern roads sometimes follow Roman routes, but not every straight road is Roman. Archaeologists identify roads through construction layers, ditches, milestones and relationships to known Roman sites.

Roman villas and the countryside

Villas were not simply Roman houses scattered randomly across Britain. They formed parts of rural economies that could include fields, animal husbandry, workshops and estate management. Some were luxurious residences designed for elite display, while others were practical farm centres. The presence of mosaics does not tell us how prosperous an entire region was. Rural archaeology must include villages, farmsteads and field systems as well as elite villas.

The military frontier as a meeting zone

Hadrian's Wall and other frontiers divided administrative systems but did not prevent interaction. Soldiers traded with civilians, local people entered military settlements, and objects moved across borders. Frontier zones could therefore be economically dynamic. Conflict was always possible, but so were negotiation and cooperation. This mixed character helps explain why archaeological evidence from frontier sites can contain objects from surprisingly distant regions.

Why AD 410 does not mean everything Roman ended

The end of Roman administration is an administrative and political change, not a switch that instantly erased Roman culture. People continued to live in Roman buildings, use Roman roads and practise Christianity. Some elites maintained connections with continental Europe. Other groups adopted new languages, burial customs and political arrangements. The fifth century was a period of transformation in which older institutions survived unevenly while new ones developed.

Reading this volume critically

The long chronological sweep can create an illusion of certainty. In reality, evidence is distributed unevenly. The prehistoric chapters contain large uncertainties because material remains preserve only fragments of past life. The Roman chapters contain more written material but also introduce source bias. A responsible reader should therefore distinguish archaeological observation, historical inference and later interpretation.

The volume also avoids treating modern borders as ancient realities. Britain, England, Scotland and Wales did not have their modern political meanings throughout prehistory or the Roman period. The names are useful for geographic orientation, but the communities who lived in these landscapes organised themselves according to local, tribal, regional and imperial structures that changed across time.

Finally, the transition from Roman Britain to the early medieval world is left open rather than forced into a dramatic collapse narrative. Volume 29 will examine the evidence for post-Roman communities, migration, Christianity and the formation of early English kingdoms. The handover between volumes is intentional: history does not stop at the edge of a chapter.

Frequently Asked Questions About Early British History

When did humans first arrive in Britain?

Evidence indicates that humans reached what is now England roughly 900,000 years ago, although occupation was not continuous across all periods.

When did Britain become an island?

Rising sea levels after the last Ice Age eventually separated Britain from continental Europe; English Heritage places the separation at about 6500 BC.

When did farming reach Britain?

Farming communities emerged during the Neolithic, beginning around the fourth millennium BC, alongside major changes in settlement, monuments and material culture.

When did the Bronze Age begin in Britain?

The Bronze Age is conventionally placed in the third millennium BC, with Beaker-associated changes around 2500 BC playing a major role in the chronology.

When was the Iron Age in Britain?

A broad conventional range is about 800 BC to AD 43, although regional chronologies vary and some Iron Age traditions continue into the early Roman period.

Did Julius Caesar conquer Britain?

No. Caesar made expeditions in 55 and 54 BC but did not establish permanent Roman rule. The full-scale conquest began under Claudius in AD 43.

When did the Roman conquest of Britain begin?

The Roman invasion began in AD 43 under Emperor Claudius. Conquest and consolidation then continued for decades.

Who was Boudica?

Boudica was the queen of the Iceni and the leader associated with a major revolt against Roman rule in AD 60–61.

When was Hadrian's Wall built?

Construction began after Emperor Hadrian's visit to Britain in AD 122 and formed part of a wider frontier system.

When did Roman Britain end?

Roman administrative rule ended in the early fifth century. AD 410 is the traditional date associated with the withdrawal of Roman administration, although the transformation was gradual.

Was Britain completely Romanised?

No. Roman rule introduced major political, economic and architectural changes, but local identities, practices and traditions continued and adapted in different regions.

Extended research dossier

One Hundred Windows into Early British History

This extended dossier deepens the narrative with focused studies. It is intentionally expansive so that the volume functions as a reference book as well as a continuous story. Each note addresses a different historical question, archaeological theme, person, place or process.

001. Climate and the changing shape of Britain

The physical shape of Britain changed repeatedly during prehistory. Glacial ice could cover northern regions, while lower sea levels exposed land now beneath the North Sea and English Channel. As ice melted, rivers changed course and coastlines moved. These environmental changes mattered because human communities were constantly adjusting where food could be obtained, where camps could be established and which routes could be travelled. A familiar modern map therefore cannot simply be projected backward into the deep past. Landscapes that look permanent to modern eyes were often temporary. Archaeologists reconstruct these lost landscapes through pollen, sediments, ancient shorelines, animal remains and geological deposits. This environmental perspective is essential to understanding why Britain was sometimes connected to continental Europe and eventually became an island.

002. Hominins before modern humans

The earliest phases of human occupation in Britain involved hominins whose lives can only be reconstructed from indirect evidence. Stone tools demonstrate technical skill, while cut marks on animal bones reveal butchery. Human remains are much rarer. The significance of sites such as Boxgrove lies in the combination of artefacts, animal bones and environmental information. Such evidence does not provide a complete narrative, but it shows that early populations could exploit large animals and manufacture tools systematically. Neanderthals later occupied Britain during suitable climatic windows. Their presence should not be imagined as a simple evolutionary stage leading automatically to modern humans. They were a long-lived human population with their own adaptive strategies, social lives and technological traditions.

003. Fire, food and survival

Control of fire transformed human possibilities in Ice Age environments. Fire provided warmth, allowed cooking, helped process materials and created a social focus around which groups could gather. Archaeological hearths are therefore important evidence of occupation, but their survival depends heavily on preservation conditions. Food strategies also changed with climate. In cold periods, large herbivores could provide substantial energy, while in warmer woodland environments smaller animals, fish and plants became more prominent. The diversity of the archaeological record demonstrates that prehistoric communities were flexible. They responded to seasonal opportunity rather than following one fixed diet. Modern ideas about agriculture or supermarkets can make hunter-gatherer life appear less organised than it actually was.

004. Caves, art and symbolic life

Caves are unusual archives because protected environments preserve organic materials, pigments and surface markings that disappear elsewhere. Creswell Crags is particularly important because it contains evidence of Palaeolithic occupation and cave art. Symbolic behaviour does not begin with Stonehenge; it is visible in much older traditions of decoration, burial and personal objects. The presence of art shows that prehistoric communities invested time in activities that cannot be explained solely by immediate survival. Interpretation remains difficult because symbols do not come with captions. Archaeologists therefore compare motifs, locations, techniques and associated remains. Every interpretation should distinguish what is directly visible from what is inferred about meaning.

005. Mesolithic wetlands

Wetlands were productive landscapes for Mesolithic communities. Water environments provided fish, birds, plants, reeds and access routes. At sites such as Star Carr, unusually good preservation allows archaeologists to reconstruct activities involving wood, antler and animal hides. Wetland sites can reveal structures that would disappear from dry sites, making them disproportionately valuable. They also illustrate a broader point: landscapes that modern society may classify as marginal were often central to prehistoric economies. The Mesolithic should therefore not be reduced to people wandering through forests. Communities could return repeatedly to favoured places, manage resources and create strong relationships with specific environments.

006. The first farmers and new social obligations

Farming introduced responsibilities that differed from those of highly mobile hunter-gatherers. Fields required preparation and maintenance. Herds required care. Surplus food could be stored, exchanged or used in communal events. These changes created new possibilities for social inequality because land, animals and stored resources could become forms of wealth. They also created opportunities for cooperation because planting, harvesting and monument construction required collective labour. Neolithic Britain consequently combined household activity with large-scale community projects. The appearance of agriculture did not automatically create kings or states, but it changed the economic foundations on which later complex societies could be built.

007. Neolithic monuments and collective memory

Monument construction created durable places where communities could remember ancestors and negotiate identity. Long barrows, henges and stone circles differed in design, but many required planned labour. The act of construction may itself have been socially important. People learned routes, assembled materials, organised food and participated in ceremonies. Monuments could therefore function as places of memory even when their practical purpose is uncertain. Their visibility also transformed the landscape: later generations encountered ancient structures and could reuse or reinterpret them. British history is thus full of locations where one period left physical traces that became part of the identity of later communities.

008. The Beaker transformation

The spread of Beaker-associated artefacts marks a major turning point. Distinctive pottery, copper objects and burial practices appeared across Britain. Ancient DNA evidence indicates that substantial population replacement occurred during this period. This does not mean one invading army conquered the island. Population change can happen through repeated migration, family movement, marriage patterns and demographic growth. The Beaker period therefore demonstrates why archaeology and genetics must be read together. Objects can spread because people move or because ideas are adopted; genetic evidence can help distinguish these possibilities, although it cannot by itself reconstruct language or social identity.

009. Metals, miners and craftsmen

Bronze required copper and tin, and obtaining these materials created networks that linked distant communities. Metalworkers needed specialised knowledge of smelting, casting and finishing. Weapons and tools were practical, but metal objects also became symbols of prestige. Hoards may represent ritual deposits, emergency concealment or accumulated wealth. Mining sites such as Grimes Graves show that extraction itself could involve specialised organisation. The Bronze Age economy was consequently more interconnected than a simple village model suggests. Specialists depended on farmers for food, while communities depended on exchange networks to obtain unusual materials. Technical skill and social organisation reinforced each other.

010. Bronze Age fields and boundaries

Field systems are among the clearest archaeological signs of changing land use. Banks, ditches and lynchets can survive long after settlements have disappeared. Such features indicate that agricultural landscapes were being divided, managed and remembered over generations. Boundaries can express ownership, practical livestock management or social distinction. They also reveal that environmental transformation was deliberate. Woodland clearance, grazing and cultivation altered ecosystems. By the later Bronze Age, people were living in landscapes increasingly shaped by human decisions, laying foundations for the more intensively managed environments encountered in the Iron Age and Roman periods.

011. Iron Age households

The Iron Age household was not simply a family living in a circular building. Houses existed within farms, villages, hillforts and territories with different scales of organisation. Roundhouses varied in size, arrangement and function. Some communities stored food in pits or granaries, while others relied on more dispersed storage. Archaeology can recover seeds, animal bones, pottery and tools that reveal domestic routines. The household was also an economic unit, producing food and crafts. Understanding ordinary settlement helps prevent history from becoming an account only of chiefs and warriors. Political power depended on thousands of people performing everyday labour.

012. Iron Age warfare and status

Weapons in graves, hoards and ritual deposits provide evidence for the importance of warfare and warrior status. Spears, swords and shields could be both practical tools and prestige objects. Chariot warfare, described by Roman authors, is also visible archaeologically, although its exact military role remains debated. Warfare did not necessarily mean constant battle. Raiding, feasting, alliance-building and displays of martial reputation could all reinforce status. Hillforts sometimes combined defensive functions with political and ceremonial roles. The Iron Age landscape therefore needs to be understood as a social system in which force, wealth and reputation were interconnected.

013. Coinage and political authority

Coinage appeared in Britain before the Roman conquest, particularly in regions with strong contacts with the continent. Coins could facilitate exchange, but they were also political statements. Rulers could place names, images and symbols on currency, making coins a portable assertion of authority. Their distribution helps archaeologists map trade, influence and political relationships. The appearance of coinage does not mean Britain had a modern monetary economy. Coins circulated within diverse systems of barter, gift exchange and taxation. Nevertheless, their presence shows that some British elites were adopting forms of political communication associated with the wider Roman world.

014. Tribal names and ancient geography

Roman authors preserved names for communities such as the Iceni, Catuvellauni, Brigantes, Silures and Trinovantes. These names are valuable but must be treated carefully. Ancient political boundaries did not necessarily correspond to modern counties, and tribal identities could change as alliances changed. A tribe may have included multiple communities and local leaders. Roman administrative divisions later reorganised the landscape again. Historians therefore use ancient names as clues rather than treating them as permanent ethnic nations. This distinction matters especially when modern national identities are projected backward onto Iron Age Britain.

015. Roman military logistics

An invasion army requires more than soldiers. It requires ships, roads, food, animals, weapons, engineering equipment, medical support and communication. Roman Britain can be understood partly through logistics because forts and roads appear where armies needed to move and supply themselves. Granaries are especially revealing: their size indicates the scale at which military communities consumed grain. River and coastal transport could reduce the burden on roads. The success of Roman conquest depended on this infrastructure as much as on battlefield discipline.

016. Richborough and the southeastern gateway

Richborough in Kent was a key entry point in the Roman conquest and later remained important as a communications centre. Its location connected Britain with continental routes. The site illustrates how conquest and commerce could use the same geography. Troops arrived through ports, while later merchants, officials and travellers used established maritime links. The Channel was therefore central to Roman Britain. Studying gateway sites shows that the province was integrated through networks rather than simply administered from distant Rome.

017. Why Roman cities were created

Roman towns served multiple purposes. They concentrated administration, markets, craft production and public ritual. Forums and basilicas created spaces where government and commerce were visible. Roads connected towns to countryside and frontier zones. Yet Roman towns did not necessarily resemble Italian cities in every respect. Local building materials, traditions and economic resources shaped their development. Town creation was therefore both imperial policy and local adaptation. The rise of urban centres changed how people encountered government, trade and public culture.

018. Public baths as social institutions

Bathhouses were technological achievements, but their importance was social. People could exercise, meet friends, conduct business and participate in communal routines. Bathing created shared experiences across provincial society, although access and quality varied. Bathhouse architecture also expressed civic ambition. Wealthy towns invested in monumental facilities because they demonstrated participation in Roman civilisation. The remains at Bath are especially famous because the hot springs provided a natural resource around which religion, urbanism and leisure could combine.

019. Roman religion in local landscapes

Roman religious practice was not limited to official temples. Household shrines, roadside offerings, springs and local sanctuaries all formed part of religious life. Provincial communities often adapted Roman forms to local gods and places. Inscriptions reveal dedications by soldiers, officials and ordinary worshippers. Some gods received both Roman and indigenous identities. This makes religion one of the clearest examples of cultural interaction in Roman Britain: imperial structures provided forms and vocabulary, while local communities supplied meanings and traditions.

020. Christianity before Constantine

Christianity existed in parts of the Roman world before it became favoured by emperors. Evidence for Christian communities in Britain is limited but significant. It includes possible symbols, religious buildings and written evidence from later periods. Scholars debate the scale of early Christian presence because material signs can be difficult to interpret. Constantine's rise changed the public environment in which Christianity operated. Over the fourth century, Christian institutions became more visible, although traditional religious practices persisted.

021. Roman law and provincial government

Roman provincial government combined military authority, taxation, courts and local administration. Governors represented imperial power, but they could not personally supervise every settlement. Local elites therefore played an essential role. Cities and civitates provided administrative frameworks through which taxes could be collected and public projects organised. Roman law created categories of status and property that influenced everyday life. Yet local customs remained important. Provincial government was a negotiation between imperial rules and the practical realities of Britain.

022. Taxes and the provincial economy

Taxation was fundamental to Roman rule. The province had to support military forces and administration, while local communities needed resources for roads and public buildings. Taxes could be collected in money or goods depending on circumstance. Agricultural production therefore mattered not simply because people needed food but because surplus supported the imperial system. Complaints about taxation could become political issues, contributing to unrest. Understanding taxes helps explain why the conquest created both opportunities for trade and burdens for provincial populations.

023. Mining and imperial wealth

Britain's mineral resources attracted Roman interest. Lead production was particularly important, and silver occurred as a by-product of some ores. Mining landscapes could involve large-scale extraction, smelting and transportation. Workers faced difficult and dangerous conditions. Mining sites also show the reach of imperial demand: material extracted in rural regions could be processed and shipped into wider economic networks. The history of Roman Britain is therefore partly a history of how local landscapes became linked to imperial resource systems.

024. Women in the archaeology of Roman Britain

Women appear less often than men in formal historical narratives, but archaeological evidence changes that balance. Jewellery, household objects, burials and inscriptions reveal family relationships and personal identities. Some women held property or participated in economic life. Elite women could sponsor religious or civic activity. Soldiers’ families created households around forts. The evidence does not support one universal female experience. Age, wealth, legal status, region and occupation all mattered. Women’s history must therefore be integrated into the provincial story rather than treated as a separate theme.

025. Children in Roman Britain

Children are archaeologically visible through toys, small shoes, burials and household material. Their mortality was high, making family continuity uncertain. Education varied by social class. Some elite boys and girls could acquire literacy, while many children entered work or household responsibilities early. Military settlements also contained families and children despite the stereotypical image of forts as exclusively male spaces. Including children changes the scale of Roman history. It shows that provinces were not inhabited only by soldiers and taxpayers but by multigenerational communities.

026. The experience of enslaved people

Slavery was a legal and economic institution in the Roman world. Enslaved people worked in households, agriculture, workshops and other settings. Their identities are difficult to recover because the sources often describe owners rather than enslaved individuals. Material evidence can nevertheless reveal conditions of labour and household organisation. A historically responsible account does not assume that slavery looked identical everywhere. Local economies and occupations varied. What was shared was the fundamental fact that enslaved people lacked the legal freedom enjoyed by citizens and many free provincials.

027. The frontier economy

Frontiers generated economic activity. Soldiers purchased food, clothing and entertainment, while civilians established shops and workshops near forts. Roads and military supply systems created demand for local products. People could travel across the frontier for trade even while military authorities sought to regulate movement. The wall was therefore not an economic dead zone. Archaeological finds of imported goods demonstrate the movement of objects across surprisingly large distances.

028. Vindolanda and the human voice

The writing tablets from Vindolanda are exceptionally valuable because they preserve fragments of ordinary correspondence. Requests for supplies, invitations and administrative messages show that military life included routine concerns. These documents remind us that the ancient past was populated by people who worried about food, clothing, family and schedules. The tablets also reveal literacy as a practical administrative tool. They provide a human-scale complement to monumental remains such as forts and walls.

029. The wall beyond the wall

Hadrian’s Wall cannot be understood as a single stone barrier. It formed part of a wider zone of roads, forts, settlements, watch points and supply systems. The landscape around it was occupied by civilians and soldiers. Crossings allowed controlled movement. The wall’s presence influenced settlement patterns on both sides. Modern tourism often focuses on the surviving stone core, but Roman engineers and soldiers were creating an entire frontier environment.

030. The Antonine Wall experiment

The Antonine Wall represents a period when Roman Britain briefly extended farther north. Built during the reign of Antoninus Pius, it demonstrates that the northern frontier was not permanently fixed. The decision to push forward required new roads, forts and supply networks. When the Romans later withdrew south, they demonstrated that strategic frontiers could be changed according to imperial priorities. Frontier history is therefore a history of decisions as much as physical walls.

031. York as a military and political centre

York developed from a Roman military foundation into one of the most important centres in Britain. Its position near major rivers and northern routes made it strategically valuable. The city supported military administration, trade and provincial government. Constantine's proclamation there in AD 306 gives York an extraordinary place in imperial history. The event shows how a distant province could become central to imperial succession at moments of crisis.

032. The breakaway emperors

Britain participated in the political instability of the later third century. The Gallic Empire included Britain for a period, while Carausius and Allectus ruled a separate regime from Britain. Such episodes demonstrate that imperial authority could fragment. They also show that Britain possessed substantial military and naval resources. A breakaway regime was possible because commanders could mobilise troops, taxation and infrastructure already established by Rome.

033. Constantius and the restoration of Roman rule

Constantius Chlorus restored Roman control in Britain in AD 296. His campaigns demonstrate that Rome could still project significant military power at the end of the third century. His death at York in AD 306 was followed by Constantine's proclamation. The sequence connects Britain's military importance to the broader Tetrarchic political system. Britain was remote from Rome geographically but remained connected to imperial decision-making through the army.

034. Constantine and the Christian empire

Constantine's reign transformed the political position of Christianity. His proclamation at York began an extraordinary career that culminated in new relationships between emperor and church. Britain became part of this transformation. Christian architecture and symbols became more visible. Yet change was uneven. Traditional religions did not disappear immediately, and rural communities could maintain older practices. The fourth century was therefore a period of religious transition rather than overnight conversion.

035. Late Roman towns

Some late Roman towns received substantial defensive walls, indicating concern about insecurity. Yet walls do not necessarily prove that urban life had ended. Market activity, crafts and administration continued in several centres. Archaeologists sometimes find evidence of repair, rebuilding and changing use of buildings. The late Roman town was often different from the earlier town: public monumental investment could decline while practical occupation continued. Urban transformation is more accurate than a simple story of urban collapse.

036. Late Roman villas and rural change

Some villas were abandoned or altered during the fourth century, while others remained prosperous. Mosaic floors, heated rooms and storage facilities show continued investment in particular estates. Regional variation is crucial. Economic conditions, security and local politics all influenced whether an estate expanded or contracted. Villa decline should therefore not be treated as a single event across Britain. Different landscapes followed different trajectories.

037. The Saxon Shore reconsidered

The term Saxon Shore comes from a late Roman list of forts and has generated substantial debate. It can be tempting to imagine a single centrally planned line built specifically against Saxons. Archaeology suggests a more complex picture. Forts may have served multiple functions, and the relationship between them and seaborne raiding evolved over time. The phrase remains useful as a conventional label but should not be allowed to obscure the uncertainty surrounding late Roman coastal defence.

038. Raiding and insecurity

Late Roman Britain faced changing patterns of external attack. Groups from across the North Sea and the northern frontier could raid coastal and rural communities. Roman authorities responded with forts, fleets, walls and military redistribution. The important point is that insecurity did not necessarily produce immediate collapse. Communities could adapt, fortify, relocate or change patterns of wealth storage. Archaeological evidence of hidden coin hoards can sometimes reflect periods of fear, although not every hoard was buried because of invasion.

039. The meaning of AD 410

AD 410 has become a convenient boundary because it is associated with the end of Roman governmental support and the instruction to British communities to defend themselves. The precise administrative circumstances remain debated. What matters is that Britain was no longer being governed through the previous imperial system. Local leaders had to assume greater responsibility. The old province became a patchwork of communities whose relationships with Rome, the continent and neighbouring peoples were changing rapidly.
