Vikings vs. Hollywood: What TV Gets Wrong (and Right) About the Norse World

Vikings. The Last Kingdom. The Northman. TV has given the Norse world a very specific look: fur, gore, and horned helmets charging out of the fog. Some of that is grounded in real history. A lot of it is Victorian invention, medieval propaganda, or straightforward Hollywood license. This volume checks the show against the sources.

I.Were the Vikings Really More Brutal Than Everyone Else?

Yes — the raids, slave-taking, and executions were genuinely violent. But historians are consistent on one point: the Vikings weren't meaningfully more brutal than the Christian kingdoms raiding and executing people right alongside them. Raiding, mutilating rivals, and displaying severed heads were standard tools of the medieval political playbook across Europe, not a uniquely Norse habit.

The clearest counter-example: Charlemagne, the celebrated Christian emperor of the Franks, ordered the execution of roughly 4,500 bound Saxon prisoners in a single day in 782 AD — the Massacre of Verden — simply for refusing to convert. Anglo-Saxon kings, meanwhile, routinely blinded rivals and cut off the hands of thieves as standard punishment.

II.Why Monks Wrote the History — and What They Left Out

Much of the Vikings' fearsome reputation comes down to who was holding the pen. Nearly all surviving accounts of early raids were written by Christian monks — the exact group the Vikings targeted when they struck the monastery at Lindisfarne in 793 AD.

Medieval Christian kings generally observed an unwritten rule: leave monasteries and unarmed clergy alone. The pagan Vikings didn't share that taboo, and the shock of that violation is a large part of why monastic chroniclers described them in such extreme, dehumanizing terms — terms that stuck for over a thousand years.

Vikings were also violent toward each other

Skeletal analysis backs this up from another angle: a study of excavated Viking-age remains in Norway found that 37% showed signs of lethal trauma, reflecting a highly decentralized society where family blood feuds made everyday life at home nearly as dangerous as a raid abroad.

"History wasn't written by the Vikings. It was written by the people they raided — which is exactly why it reads the way it does."

III.Was Ragnar Lothbrok a Real Person?

Partly. A historical Viking leader named Reginherus is recorded raiding Paris in 845 AD, and some historians link him to the legendary figure. But the "Ragnar Lothbrok" of television and Norse sagas is best understood as a composite character — closer to King Arthur than to a single, precisely documented man. Old Norse sagas blended multiple real raiders and folklore into one legendary figure long after the events they describe.

IV.Is Erling Haaland Actually a Viking?

Not literally — "Viking" was a job description (fara í víking, "to go raiding"), not an ethnicity. But in the ancestral sense, Norwegian footballer Erling Haaland is about as close as it gets. He was born to Norwegian parents from Bryne, in the historic heartland of Norse raiders, and his surname derives from Old Norse words meaning "high land."

The physical resemblance isn't just internet meme material

Genetic and skeletal evidence from Norse burial sites shows that high-status Viking chieftains — heavily fed on meat and dairy, unlike the average Viking farmer — often reached well over six feet tall, with rugged, square facial structure. Haaland, at 6-foot-5 with a prominent jaw and long blond hair, fits that upper-tier Norse physical profile closely, which is exactly why the comparison keeps circulating.

V.The Hygiene Myth-Buster: Why English Women Preferred Viking Men

Medieval chronicler John of Wallingford left a famously irritated account of Danish settlers in England "stealing" the affections of local women — and the historical explanation is almost mundane: hygiene.

That combination of cleanliness and mercantile wealth led to widespread intermarriage across the Danelaw — the swath of England under Norse control — and modern genetic studies confirm meaningful Scandinavian DNA persisting in the British population today.

VI.The Horned Helmet, and Other Things Hollywood Invented

The single most famous "Viking" image — the horned helmet — is a 19th-century Romantic-era invention, popularized by costume designers for a Wagner opera, not something any actual Norse warrior wore into battle. No archaeological find has ever produced a genuine Viking-age horned battle helmet.

TV also tends to imagine a unified "Viking Empire" marching under one banner. In reality, Scandinavian society was highly decentralized, and Norse raiders spent as much energy fighting each other in blood feuds as they spent raiding abroad.

A note on sources: much of the written record of the Viking Age comes from the people the Vikings raided, which is worth keeping in mind whenever a source's tone turns unusually vivid.

VII.Frequently Asked Questions

Were Vikings more violent than other medieval peoples?

Not meaningfully. Historians generally agree Vikings weren't more brutal than the Christian kingdoms raiding and executing people around them — Charlemagne's forces, for example, killed thousands of bound prisoners in a single day in 782 AD.

Is Ragnar Lothbrok based on a real person?

Partly. A historical raider named Reginherus attacked Paris in 845 AD, but the legendary "Ragnar Lothbrok" of the sagas and TV is widely considered a composite figure blending multiple real raiders with folklore.

Did Vikings really wear horned helmets?

No. The horned helmet is a 19th-century theatrical invention, popularized by opera costume design. No genuine Viking-age battle helmet with horns has ever been found archaeologically.

Is Erling Haaland descended from Vikings?

In the general ancestral sense, yes — his Norwegian family comes from Bryne, in the historic heartland of Norse raiders, and his surname derives from Old Norse. "Viking" itself, however, was a job title rather than an ethnicity.

Why did English women reportedly prefer Viking men?

Medieval chronicles suggest Viking hygiene — including a weekly bathing tradition called Laugardagr — and their trade wealth made them more appealing than local Anglo-Saxon men, leading to widespread intermarriage in Viking-controlled England.
