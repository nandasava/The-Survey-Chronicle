INTRODUCTION

Before There Was England

A kingdom had to be made before it could be inherited

Volume 28 followed the peoples and landscapes of Britain through prehistory and Roman rule. This volume begins after Roman administration has disappeared and follows the centuries in which the political communities later called Anglo-Saxon kingdoms developed, fought, converted and transformed themselves. The period from 597 to 927 is one of the decisive stretches of British and English history because it contains the formation of institutions that later medieval England would inherit.

Yet the story must not be reduced to a simple sequence of kings. The English kingdom grew out of relationships between Britons, Angles, Saxons, Jutes, Franks, Irish Christians, Scandinavians and many local communities whose identities cannot always be recovered from surviving sources. The term 'Anglo-Saxon' is useful for the language and much of the political culture of the period, but it should not be treated as a single race or a uniform people. The kingdom that emerged in the tenth century was a product of centuries of interaction.

This volume is deliberately expansive. It follows royal history while also examining agriculture, slavery, law, monasteries, literacy, warfare, trade, coinage, women, towns, archaeology, religion and the changing map. The central theme is state formation: how scattered kingdoms became a single English monarchy without losing the regional identities that had shaped Britain for generations.

CHAPTER 01

1. The World of the Four Kingdoms

597–650

The volume opens with the political map that Augustine encountered. Kent, Mercia, Northumbria and East Anglia were not provinces of an existing England but separate kingdoms with shifting boundaries, rival royal families and networks of tribute. The word England is useful retrospectively, but it can hide how fragmented power was in the seventh century. Kent had unusually strong links with Frankish Europe; Northumbria grew from Bernicia and Deira; Mercia expanded through the Midlands; East Anglia controlled fertile eastern country and important routes to the North Sea. West Saxon power existed in the south-west, while Sussex and Essex had their own rulers. The key question was not simply who owned which county. Early medieval kings competed for status, alliances, access to markets, skilled metalworkers, religious prestige and the right to receive tribute. A ruler who was recognised as an overlord could exercise authority beyond the territory he directly administered, but that authority could weaken after a military defeat or a dynastic death.

The sources for this world are uneven. Bede, writing in Northumbria in the early eighth century, provides the most famous narrative framework for the conversion of the English, but archaeology often reveals places, objects and economic activity that written sources barely mention. Royal genealogies preserve claims about ancestry, while law codes show later kings trying to make social distinctions more precise. In this landscape, Christianity became both a religion and a channel of diplomacy. Marriage alliances, missionary activity and the exchange of gifts could all connect kingdoms without eliminating their independence. The history of the period therefore begins with a mosaic rather than a state.

Later English political institutions grew from this environment. The royal household was mobile; a court was more often a travelling centre of decision-making than a permanent capital. The king relied on household warriors and regional elites. Landholding, protection, food supply and the ability to reward followers mattered as much as battlefield strength. When historians describe a seventh-century king as powerful, they are describing a political capacity assembled through people and relationships rather than a modern bureaucracy. Understanding this distinction helps explain why some rulers could dominate several kingdoms for a time and yet leave little permanent machinery behind them.

The making of England was a process of power, belief, settlement, law, exchange and memory—not a single event.
CHAPTER 02

2. Augustine and the Mission of 597

597

In 597 a mission sent by Pope Gregory the Great reached Kent under Augustine. Later tradition attached a single dramatic moment to the mission, but conversion was a process that unfolded through royal decisions, household networks, clergy and changing religious practice. King Æthelberht of Kent was especially significant because his wife Bertha, a Frankish Christian princess, already worshipped as a Christian. Canterbury provided the mission with a durable base, and the establishment of a bishopric there helped connect the English kingdoms to the wider Latin Christian world.

The mission also demonstrates how religion operated through political geography. Augustine did not arrive in an empty spiritual landscape. Christian communities survived from the Roman period, particularly in western Britain, even though the religious institutions of Roman Britannia had changed dramatically after the withdrawal of imperial administration. The mission associated with Augustine represented a renewed connection to the church of Rome and to the written, episcopal and monastic culture that was developing across post-Roman Europe. Kent's location close to continental routes mattered: merchants, diplomats and clerics could move between southern Britain and Gaul more easily than many later maps suggest.

Æthelberht's conversion did not mean every Kentish subject immediately became Christian. Bede describes a royal process in which a king could permit missionaries to preach, while existing religious practice continued. Churches could become royal institutions and bishops could become advisers, record keepers and diplomatic figures. The conversion of kings mattered because rulers controlled patronage, assemblies and access to resources, but it still had to be translated into local communities. Burial archaeology, church sites and changing artefact assemblages show that belief and identity did not shift overnight.

The long-term significance of 597 is therefore greater than the date itself. It created a narrative starting point for what later writers called the conversion of England and established Canterbury as a central ecclesiastical site. It also introduced a model in which royal power, written religion and international connections could reinforce each other. The process would be repeated, resisted and revised in other kingdoms over the next century.

CHAPTER 03

3. Kent Under Æthelberht and the Frankish Connection

late 6th–early 7th century

Kent occupied a strategic position at the eastern entrance to Britain. Its ports and proximity to Gaul helped its kings participate in continental exchange, while its control of routes into the Thames valley gave it influence beyond its immediate territory. Æthelberht is often presented as one of the first great Anglo-Saxon overlords, and the evidence for his authority is strengthened by his connection to Bertha and by the legal code attributed to him. That law code is significant because it is among the earliest surviving examples of written English law, written in Old English using the Latin alphabet.

The provisions of Æthelberht's laws reveal a society where personal status, kinship, property and compensation were central to public order. The law did not create a modern criminal code. Instead, it specified payments and protections that could regulate disputes and reduce cycles of retaliation. The detailed distinction between the status of different people demonstrates how early English rulers used law to make hierarchy visible. The church was included within this legal world, suggesting that Christian institutions were being incorporated into royal authority rather than operating in isolation.

Kent's later political decline is equally instructive. Royal power depended upon succession, alliances and external circumstances. As other kingdoms expanded, Kent's position as an early centre of Christianity did not automatically guarantee political supremacy. Mercia and Northumbria developed larger territorial power, while Wessex became increasingly important in the south. Kent nonetheless remained an important cultural region. Canterbury's ecclesiastical institutions preserved texts and artistic traditions, and its links with Frankish Europe helped create an environment in which manuscripts, luxury goods and religious ideas could circulate.

The story of Kent introduces a recurring pattern in the whole period: an early leader can pioneer an institution without becoming the permanent centre of the political system. Christianity, law, literacy and diplomacy became broader features of English kingdoms precisely because they were adopted in different political settings.

CHAPTER 04

4. Edwin of Northumbria and the Rise of a Northern Power

616–632

Northumbria emerged from the union of two northern political centres, Bernicia and Deira. Edwin, king from 616 to 632, extended his authority over a wide area and was remembered by Bede as an exceptional ruler. His reign demonstrates how early medieval kingship could combine conquest, dynastic politics, Christianity and control of communications. The royal household moved through the realm, and the king's authority was expressed by the ability to command warriors, settle disputes, and receive recognition from subordinate rulers.

Edwin's conversion to Christianity became one of the most famous episodes in Bede's history. Paulinus, a Roman missionary associated with Canterbury, worked at the Northumbrian court, while Edwin's Christian queen Æthelburg provided a dynastic and religious connection to Kent. Bede's account of the council in which Edwin considered Christianity is partly theological narrative and partly political history. The famous comparison of human life to a sparrow flying through a warm hall captures the uncertainty of existence, while the discussion of belief reveals how kings could use religious deliberation as a public and political act.

Northumbrian power was not secure. Edwin's defeat and death at Hatfield Chase in 633 shattered his supremacy. The disaster allowed rivals to reassert themselves and opened a period of religious uncertainty. Yet the institutions associated with Northumbria survived. Monasteries such as Lindisfarne became major centres of scholarship and art, and Northumbrian rulers later supported an intellectual culture that produced some of the most important manuscripts and theological writings of early medieval Britain.

The geography of Northumbria also explains its importance. It connected Britain to the North Sea, the Irish Sea and the northern frontier. Political relationships reached toward the Picts, Britons and Scots as well as other English kingdoms. The northern story cannot therefore be reduced to a line of English kings. It is a history of overlapping political cultures in which Northumbria could at different moments be the strongest force in Britain and, at other moments, a kingdom under intense external pressure.

CHAPTER 05

5. Oswald, Oswiu and the Northumbrian Christian Revival

634–670

Oswald succeeded in restoring Northumbrian power after the death of Edwin, but his religious legacy was at least as influential as his political one. After years of exile, Oswald returned with a connection to the monastery of Iona and supported Aidan, the Irish monk who founded a monastic community at Lindisfarne. The resulting network helped Christianise parts of northern England and created a distinctive intellectual tradition. Iona and Lindisfarne were not merely places of worship; they were communities of learning, manuscript production, diplomacy and elite burial.

Oswald's death at Maserfield in 642 showed how rapidly political fortunes could change. His successor Oswiu later defeated Penda of Mercia at the battle of the Winwaed in 655, bringing Northumbrian influence to a new height. Oswiu also governed an era of ecclesiastical debate. The differences between Roman and Irish practices, especially in calculating the date of Easter and in aspects of clerical custom, became politically significant because Christian kingdoms needed coherent structures of authority. The Synod of Whitby in 664 is therefore best understood not only as a theological meeting but also as an event in the consolidation of church organisation.

The Northumbrian church produced extraordinary cultural achievements. The Lindisfarne Gospels, although later than the reigns of Oswald and Oswiu, belongs to a tradition of insular art shaped by Christian texts, Celtic influences, Anglo-Saxon metalwork and manuscript craftsmanship. Wearmouth-Jarrow, founded under the patronage of kings, became a centre for Bede's scholarship. Such institutions preserved classical learning and gave the English kingdoms access to a written culture that extended far beyond local political boundaries.

Northumbrian history also reveals that conversion did not erase older identities. People continued to express status through burial goods, kinship and military loyalty. Kings presented themselves as Christian rulers while retaining the social language of warriors and gift giving. The result was a hybrid political culture: Christian in institutional identity, but still deeply rooted in aristocratic competition, warfare and dynastic prestige.

CHAPTER 06

6. Penda and the Power of Mercia

c. 626–655

Penda of Mercia became one of the most formidable rulers of seventh-century Britain. Unlike Northumbrian kings who promoted Christianity, Penda remained a prominent pagan ruler, yet his political relationships crossed religious boundaries. His children and successors would later become associated with Christian foundations, illustrating that dynastic politics could outlast individual religious choices. Penda's military campaigns reached into Northumbria, East Anglia and the Welsh borderlands. His importance lies partly in showing that Mercia was already capable of acting as a major power before its eighth-century expansion.

Mercia's geography was a strategic advantage. The Midlands contained fertile river valleys, major routeways and connections between eastern and western Britain. Its rulers could interact with Welsh kingdoms to the west, Northumbria to the north, East Anglia to the east and Wessex to the south. A Mercian king who controlled the central zone could pressure several neighbours simultaneously. Yet Mercian authority, like that of other early kingdoms, was not the same as modern territorial government. Its reach varied by region and depended on tribute and allegiance.

Penda's death at the Winwaed did not end Mercia. Instead, Mercian kings adapted to a new Christian political environment. Peada, a son of Penda, accepted Christianity, and later Mercian rulers patronised monasteries. The conversion of Mercia was gradual, contested and tied to court politics. It also helped establish the kingdom as a cultural centre. Monastic foundations preserved genealogies, charters and liturgical traditions, while royal patronage supported churches and land grants.

Mercia would become the dominant kingdom of the eighth century under rulers such as Æthelbald and Offa, but its earlier history is essential. The kingdom's rise was built on a combination of geographic centrality, military organisation, agricultural resources and an increasingly sophisticated relationship between kings and religious institutions. The seventh century was therefore not an isolated prologue. It created the political geography from which later Mercian expansion emerged.

CHAPTER 07

7. East Anglia, Sutton Hoo and the Archaeology of Kingship

7th century

East Anglia offers one of the clearest demonstrations of why archaeology matters to early English history. The burial complex at Sutton Hoo revealed a world of long-distance exchange, elite display and connections across the North Sea. The famous ship burial contained objects whose materials and styles linked eastern England with Scandinavia and the wider continental world. The burial does not provide a simple identity card for a named king, and interpretations have changed, but its scale shows that early East Anglian elites possessed the resources and networks needed to commission extraordinary monuments.

East Anglia was a kingdom divided historically into northern and southern areas, with access to river systems, the Wash and North Sea routes. Its economy included agriculture, animal husbandry, craft production and trade. Imported goods could arrive through coastal networks that linked the region to Francia and Scandinavia. The kingdom's rulers also faced pressure from Northumbria and Mercia, and its political fortunes were repeatedly altered by stronger neighbours.

Christianity arrived through multiple channels. Rædwald is famous because Bede describes him as having a pagan altar alongside a Christian one, illustrating that conversion could be negotiated rather than absolute. Later East Anglian kings supported churches and monasteries, but the persistence of mixed practice reminds historians that belief was lived at household and community level. The elite burials that survive in the archaeological record cannot simply be labelled pagan or Christian without considering context.

Sutton Hoo also changes the language of 'Anglo-Saxon invasion'. The seventh-century elite was not culturally isolated from the continent. Styles, metals, weapons and symbols moved across the North Sea. The sea was not merely a barrier; it was a highway. East Anglia's history therefore belongs to a larger North Sea world in which Britain, Francia and Scandinavia were already connected long before the later Viking Age.

CHAPTER 08

8. The Kingdoms Behind the Name England

7th century

The collective phrase Anglo-Saxon kingdoms describes political communities that could be fiercely independent. Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Kent, Essex, Sussex and Wessex are often grouped as the 'Heptarchy', but the term was created by later historians and should not be treated as a fixed constitutional system. Some kingdoms split, merged or disappeared; others exercised overlordship across several regions. Lindsey, Hwicce and other smaller political communities could also matter greatly at particular moments.

The idea of a fixed seven-kingdom map can obscure the importance of borderlands. The Severn valley, the Welsh Marches, the southern coast and the Humber region were zones of contact and conflict. Brittonic-speaking communities survived in western and northern regions, while Anglo-Saxon settlement expanded unevenly. Some regions were multilingual and culturally mixed. Archaeological evidence often shows adaptation rather than sudden replacement.

Royal genealogies helped rulers claim legitimacy. Descent from Woden appears in several Anglo-Saxon royal pedigrees, and Christian writers later adapted these traditions into written histories. Such genealogies were political documents. They established the antiquity of a dynasty and connected living rulers to legendary ancestors. The conversion to Christianity did not immediately erase pre-Christian royal symbolism; instead, Christian kingship incorporated older ideas about sacred ancestry, gift giving and the king's relationship with his people.

The political unit that would become England was therefore constructed slowly. It required military conquest, marriage alliances, religious networks, written administration and shared cultural forms. Later kings could claim to be kings of all the English because generations of rulers had already created habits of diplomacy and overlordship. The unity of England was not inevitable; it was an historical achievement built through repeated crises.

CHAPTER 09

9. The Conversion of Sussex and Essex

7th century

Some kingdoms accepted Christianity earlier than others. Essex and Sussex demonstrate why conversion was not a one-way march. Missionaries could establish churches in a kingdom, only for royal support to be withdrawn or pagan traditions to regain public visibility. In Essex, the conversion associated with Mellitus did not prevent later reversion. In Sussex, Christianisation was connected to the activity of Wilfrid after political changes created an opening for renewed missionary work.

These experiences underline the importance of rulers. A king who accepted baptism could provide land, protection and access to a royal population, but his successors were not bound to continue the same policy. Christianity needed institutions that could survive dynastic change. Monasteries, bishops, churches and clerical estates gradually created such continuity. The land grants supporting religious foundations also tied the church to the economic structure of kingship, because productive estates supplied food and labour.

The southern kingdoms also reveal the importance of external connections. Kent's links to Francia, Sussex's coastal geography and Essex's position on the Thames corridor all brought political and commercial contacts. Christian missionaries travelled along some of the same routes used by merchants and aristocrats. The religious transformation of southern Britain therefore occurred in a social landscape already shaped by mobility.

CHAPTER 10

10. Wessex Before Alfred

7th–8th centuries

Wessex began as one southern kingdom among several, but its geographic position and dynastic continuity later made it the foundation of English unification. The early West Saxon kings operated in a landscape that included the Thames valley, the south coast, the upper Thames and the western border with Brittonic kingdoms. Their power expanded and contracted in response to Mercian pressure.

The conversion of Wessex is associated with figures such as Birinus and King Cynegils. Winchester became an important ecclesiastical and royal centre, although it should not be imagined as a permanent capital in the later medieval sense. The court continued to move, and royal administration depended on estates and local elites.

The eighth century was difficult for Wessex because Mercian kings achieved extensive overlordship. Æthelbald and Offa projected power across much of southern England. Offa's Dyke, although its exact chronology and functions remain subjects of debate, illustrates the scale of Mercian investment in frontier defence and political display. Wessex was therefore often operating from a position of pressure. The later rise of King Ecgberht changed the balance and gave the West Saxon dynasty a platform for expansion.

This background matters when Alfred appears in the ninth century. His success was not produced from nothing. It depended on a royal house with established claims, a network of estates, traditions of military mobilisation, relations with Mercian elites and a geographical core that could still be defended when other kingdoms collapsed.

CHAPTER 11

11. Æthelbald and Offa: Mercia at its Height

716–796

Mercia reached remarkable political strength in the eighth century. Æthelbald presented himself in charters as ruler of a wide territory, and Offa later became the most famous Mercian king of the period. Offa's authority extended into parts of the south, and his coins display both technical skill and an understanding of kingship as a public financial institution. The famous gold dinar associated with Offa demonstrates the reach of Islamic coin designs into Anglo-Saxon England, although the coin's exact purpose is debated.

Offa's reign also shows the sophistication of diplomacy. Marriage alliances connected dynasties, while relations with Charlemagne were important enough to generate correspondence and negotiation. A proposed marriage between Offa's son Ecgfrith and a daughter of Charlemagne did not proceed as planned, but the exchanges demonstrate that Mercia was recognised as a substantial European kingdom. The presence of foreign merchants and the movement of luxury objects confirm that English elites were integrated into wider networks.

The kingdom's administrative organisation became more structured. Royal charters recorded land grants, monasteries accumulated substantial estates, and elite centres such as Tamworth developed as places of assembly and production. Yet Mercian power had limits. After Offa's death in 796, political instability and dynastic weakness reduced Mercian dominance, while Wessex gradually re-emerged as the strongest southern kingdom.

The Mercian century left a lasting inheritance. English political culture retained Mercian place-names, law traditions, manuscript practices and aristocratic families. When later West Saxon kings incorporated Mercia, they did not simply replace its institutions. They inherited and adapted them. England's eventual unification was thus partly a merger of political traditions.

CHAPTER 12

12. Charlemagne, Francia and the English Courts

8th century

Anglo-Saxon kings were active participants in continental politics. The Frankish empire under Charlemagne transformed western Europe, and English rulers had to navigate a powerful neighbour across the Channel. Diplomatic exchange involved letters, gifts, ecclesiastical reform and marriage negotiations. Charlemagne and Offa are especially important because their correspondence reveals that English rulers considered themselves actors in a wider European political system.

The channel between England and Francia carried more than diplomats. Coins, manuscripts, clerics and artistic styles travelled in both directions. English monasteries had intellectual connections with the Frankish world, while Carolingian scholars knew of English teachers and books. Alcuin of York became one of the most influential intellectuals at Charlemagne's court, carrying Northumbrian scholarship into the heart of the Carolingian renaissance.

These connections affected royal government. Literacy was not a modern bureaucracy, but the ability to produce charters, law codes and ecclesiastical records expanded the reach of institutions. Latin became a language of international communication, while Old English could be used for local law and royal instruction. The bilingual character of government helped Anglo-Saxon rulers connect local society with continental Christianity.

CHAPTER 13

13. Alcuin and the Intellectual World of Northumbria

8th century

Northumbria produced an intellectual culture of international importance. Bede, born around 673 and based at Wearmouth-Jarrow, wrote histories, biblical commentaries, scientific works and educational texts. His Ecclesiastical History of the English People became the foundational narrative for later historians of Anglo-Saxon Christianity. Yet Bede's work should be read critically: he was a Christian monk writing with theological goals, and his surviving knowledge often depended on correspondents, oral traditions and earlier texts.

Alcuin inherited this scholarly world. His move from York to Charlemagne's court shows how ideas travelled. He participated in educational reform and helped establish standards for reading, copying texts and teaching clergy. Anglo-Saxon manuscripts were therefore part of a larger European movement to preserve and organise learning.

The monasteries that created this intellectual culture depended on land, labour and royal protection. Learning was embedded in economic power. Scriptoria required parchment, ink, trained scribes and time. Libraries could contain biblical books, classical authors and historical narratives, while monasteries also managed estates. The survival of the written record is thus inseparable from the institutions that controlled resources.

The period's manuscripts also demonstrate cultural creativity. Insular art combined interlace, animal ornament and Christian imagery, while later manuscripts could incorporate continental styles. The result was not a passive preservation of Rome but a new visual and literary culture that would influence medieval Europe.

CHAPTER 14

14. The Northumbrian Golden Age and its Decline

700–800

Northumbria's cultural brilliance existed alongside political instability. Kings competed for the throne, dynasties split, and Mercia increasingly challenged Northumbrian power. By the late eighth century, political influence had shifted southward. The decline did not erase Northumbrian culture, but it meant that its monasteries became vulnerable to raids and political disruption.

The famous raid on Lindisfarne in 793 is often used as the conventional beginning of the Viking Age in England. It was shocking to contemporaries because a sacred island monastery was attacked, but it was also part of a wider pattern of Scandinavian raiding across the Irish Sea and continental coasts. The raid did not immediately destroy Northumbria. Instead, it became an enduring symbol of a new kind of threat.

CHAPTER 15

15. The First Viking Raids

793–835

Scandinavian raiders began attacking Britain before the arrival of the large armies that would transform English politics. Coastal monasteries were attractive targets because they held valuable metalwork, food and other resources, and because their populations were not organised primarily for military defence. The raids were not always purely destructive. Vikings also traded, captured people, gathered intelligence and established routes.

The chronology matters. Between the late eighth century and the 830s, attacks could be opportunistic and intermittent. By the 830s, larger fleets were appearing, and by the 850s some forces were wintering in Britain. That change transformed the political problem for Anglo-Saxon kings. A raid could be answered by a local levy; a mobile army spending a winter near a river system required sustained mobilisation.

The Vikings exploited the waterways that had long connected English regions. The Thames, Humber, Trent and other navigable rivers became routes for both invasion and trade. Their ships could carry crews quickly between coastal zones and river corridors, while shallow drafts allowed movement farther inland than many defenders expected. The geography of the Viking wars was therefore also the geography of rivers, ports and old Roman roads.

16. Ecgberht of Wessex and the New Southern Balance

802–839

Ecgberht's reign marked a major step in the rise of Wessex. After years of pressure from Mercia, he defeated King Beornwulf at Ellandun in 825. The victory weakened Mercian dominance in the south and allowed Wessex to acquire influence in Kent, Surrey, Sussex and Essex. This did not yet create England, but it altered the political balance decisively.

Ecgberht also faced Viking attacks and a combined force of Vikings and Cornish at Hingston Down in 835. His ability to mobilise against enemies from both sea and west shows the increasingly complex defence environment of the ninth century. His descendants would inherit a more powerful kingdom precisely when Scandinavian armies became larger and more persistent.

17. Æthelwulf and the West Saxon Dynasty

839–858

King Æthelwulf continued the dynasty's consolidation. He divided responsibilities within the royal family and maintained relations with the church. His pilgrimage to Rome and the marriage of his son Alfred's sister to a continental ruler strengthened connections beyond Britain.

The importance of Æthelwulf lies partly in family strategy. The West Saxon monarchy increasingly relied on carefully managed succession and the cooperation of brothers and sons. That flexibility became vital in the Viking crisis. The dynasty survived repeated deaths and defeats because it could produce rulers with overlapping claims and military experience.

CHAPTER 18

18. The Great Heathen Army Arrives

865

The arrival of the Great Heathen Army in 865 changed the scale of the Viking challenge. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle uses a memorable phrase for the force, but modern historians avoid imagining one permanent army with a single fixed composition. It was a coalition of warriors whose size, leadership and membership could change. Archaeological evidence from places such as Repton and Torksey shows the scale and organisation of winter camps.

The army first operated in East Anglia, where King Edmund reached an agreement with the Vikings. It then moved north, overthrowing the Northumbrian kingdom's political order, and later turned toward Mercia. By 871, Wessex faced the central pressure of the war. The collapse of three major kingdoms created an entirely new strategic environment.

The Viking forces were not simply destroyers. Many settled, farmed and integrated into local economies. The later Danelaw was a cultural landscape with Scandinavian place-names, burial traditions, legal practices and mixed communities. Conflict and settlement were therefore connected processes.

CHAPTER 19

19. Northumbria Falls to the Vikings

866–867

York became one of the key prizes of the Viking wars. The city had inherited the commercial importance of Roman Eboracum and remained a major centre in the early medieval period. Control of York offered access to northern trade routes, agricultural wealth and political legitimacy. The Viking capture of the city in 866 was therefore more than a military episode.

It was Roman in its use of towns, roads and Christianity; Germanic in its dynastic and military culture; Brittonic in the surviving peoples and landscapes of the west and north; Irish and Northumbrian in its monastic connections; Frankish and Carolingian in intellectual and diplomatic exchange; and Scandinavian in the settlements, place-names, commerce and political traditions that became embedded in the tenth-century kingdom.

This conclusion is the bridge to Volume 30. The next volume follows the Viking Age in even greater depth, from the first raids through the Great Heathen Army, the Danelaw, Scandinavian kings, the return of Viking invasions and the crisis that culminated in 1066.

The making of England was a process of power, belief, settlement, law, exchange and memory—not a single event.
