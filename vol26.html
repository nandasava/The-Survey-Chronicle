Tap your phone to pay at a coffee shop, and you probably assume Google just handled that transaction. It didn't. Google Pay never touches, moves, or clears your money — your bank, Visa or Mastercard, and the merchant's actual payment processor do all of that. But step inside Google's own Play Store, and the picture flips entirely: there, Google is the sole processor, judge, and toll collector, taking a cut as high as 30% on every digital sale. This volume untangles both halves of that story.

I.Google Pay Is a Wallet, Not a Payment Processor

When you tap to pay in a store or click Google Pay at checkout, Google acts strictly as a secure middleman. It uses a technique called tokenization: your real card number is masked with a virtual placeholder, which then travels across standard rails — Visa, Mastercard, American Express — to be authorized directly by your bank.

For online checkouts, Google Pay hands that encrypted token to the merchant's own payment service provider, such as Stripe, Adyen, or Worldpay, which actually settles the transaction. In markets like India, Google Pay instead plugs into local bank-to-bank rails like the UPI network via the NPCI, bypassing card networks entirely.

In short: Google Pay stores the card. It doesn't move the money.

II.Inside the Play Store: Where Google Actually Is the Processor

The moment you buy an app, rent a movie, or subscribe to Google One, the rules change completely. Google Payment Corp. (GPC), a wholly owned Alphabet subsidiary, becomes the Merchant of Record. Google's internal Play Billing system logs the sale, collects the funds directly, and only then pays the developer their share.

That share is the crux of years of legal conflict: Google has historically taken a 15% to 30% service fee on digital goods and subscriptions sold through the Play Store — a rate roughly ten times what a public payment processor like Stripe charges for the equivalent transaction.

III.Why Google Has Never Built a Public Stripe Competitor

Google has the engineering resources to build a public payment service provider tomorrow. It hasn't, for reasons that are more strategic than technical:

Regulatory weight

Operating as a public PSP for outside businesses would mean acquiring money transmitter licenses across dozens of jurisdictions and taking on Anti-Money-Laundering and Know-Your-Customer compliance for millions of unverified merchants — a massive liability if any of them turn out to be fraudulent.

Fraud and chargeback labor

Public processors absorb enormous manual overhead resolving merchant disputes and chargebacks. That's a labor-heavy business model that doesn't fit Google's preference for highly automated, software-scaled products.

A better deal already exists

By staying a wallet rather than a processor, Google lets Stripe, PayPal, and Square absorb the regulatory risk on the open web — while reserving its own closed, high-margin billing system exclusively for Play Store and YouTube transactions, where the 15–30% cut vastly outperforms a public processor's thin 2.9% margin.

"Google doesn't need to compete with Stripe when it can just own the only tollbooth on its own road."

IV.Epic v. Google and the Fight to Open the Ecosystem

That closed tollbooth has drawn sustained antitrust pressure. In the landmark Epic Games v. Google case, courts ruled against Google's practice of forcing all Android developers to use its own billing system, and ordered the Play Store ecosystem opened to competition.

In Europe, the Digital Markets Act treats Google's self-preferencing of its own payment system as exactly the kind of anti-competitive behavior it was designed to prevent, pushing Google to allow outside processors like Stripe or Shopify to handle Android transactions directly rather than routing everything through Google Payment Corp.

The practical result: Google is now legally required, in a growing number of markets, to let developers route around its own payment system — chipping away at one of Alphabet's highest-margin internal businesses.

VI.Frequently Asked Questions

Does Google Pay process my payment directly?

No. Google Pay tokenizes your card and passes it to your bank, card network, or the merchant's payment processor (like Stripe or Adyen), which actually clears and settles the transaction.

Why doesn't Google have its own PSP like Stripe?

Operating a public payment processor requires heavy regulatory licensing, fraud and chargeback management, and thin profit margins compared to Google's existing high-margin closed billing system inside the Play Store.

How much does Google take from Play Store sales?

Google has historically charged a service fee of 15% to 30% on digital goods and subscriptions sold through Google Play, compared to roughly 2.9% for a typical outside payment processor.

What did the Epic Games v. Google ruling change?

Courts ruled against Google's requirement that all Android developers use its own billing system, forcing Google to allow outside payment processors into the Play Store ecosystem.

Does Google Payment Corp. exist?

Yes. Google Payment Corp. is Alphabet's internal payment subsidiary, but it is used almost exclusively to process transactions within Google's own products, such as Play Store purchases and YouTube subscriptions.
