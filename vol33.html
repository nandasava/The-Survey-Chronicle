Before the map had borders

There is a familiar picture of human history: Africa at one end of a line, a migration arrow crossing the Red Sea, another arrow through Asia, and finally a world filled with people. It is useful as a first sketch. It is also incomplete.

Vol. 33 follows the evidence beneath that sketch. It asks how Homo sapiens emerged within Africa; how populations moved through changing landscapes; how earlier humans and other hominins remained part of the story; how genetics records both separation and contact; how land bridges and sea crossings opened routes; and how later societies transformed population histories into categories called race.

The volume also makes room for a creature that seems, at first, to belong to another book: Gomphotherium. In these pages, “the Gompht” is simply a readable nickname for that extinct proboscidean genus. It was not a human ancestor. It matters because human beings did not evolve in an empty landscape. They shared changing ecosystems with large mammals whose fossils can help reconstruct the worlds through which humans moved.

How to read this volume: A claim can be established, inferred, or proposed. The strongest sections tell you which is which. When the evidence is incomplete, this volume says so rather than filling the gap with a confident story.

Timeline

Dates below are deliberately broad. Deep-time evidence is often reported as ranges, and different dating methods can produce different uncertainty intervals. The timeline is a reading guide, not a claim that history happened in ten perfectly separated steps.

4+ million yearsBipedalism is established in several early hominins.
~2 million yearsEarly Homo populations are moving beyond Africa into parts of Eurasia.
~300,000 yearsHomo sapiens is represented by early fossils including Jebel Irhoud.
~335,000–236,000 yearsHomo naledi occupies southern Africa, overlapping in time with early Homo sapiens.
~70,000–60,000 yearsA major demographic expansion contributes to the peopling of regions outside Africa.
~50,000 yearsGenetic evidence indicates substantial periods of contact and interbreeding between Homo sapiens and Neanderthals.
Last Ice AgeBeringia forms a large connected ecological region as sea levels fall.

Past ~12,000 yearsFood production, sedentary settlements and large-scale environmental modification accelerate in several regions.

Story 001 · Part I

The Wrong Question: Where Did the First Human Live?

Before the First Person

The story begins with a deceptively simple question: deep time, definitions, evidence, and the question of what it means for a species to have an origin. In human-origins research, the temptation is to turn a complicated record into one clean arrow: one place, one people, one departure, one destination. The evidence rarely behaves that way. Fossils are separated by thousands or millions of years; archaeological layers are incomplete; genomes preserve some histories while losing others; and landscapes that look like barriers today could have been very different during colder or wetter periods. Vol. 33 therefore treats movement as a process rather than a single event. A population could split, reconnect, disappear, return, exchange genes, adopt a technology, or leave no recognizable trace at all. The goal is not to make the past mysterious for its own sake. It is to show where the evidence is strong, where it is provisional, and why the distinction matters.

One anchor for this investigation is the evidence summarized by Smithsonian. Homo sapiens evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago; human evolution extends roughly six million years. That point places a boundary around what can responsibly be claimed. It does not automatically answer every question attached to it. A fossil can demonstrate that a population was present; it cannot by itself reveal the language spoken around the fire. A genetic pattern can reveal relatedness and population movement; it cannot supply a complete map of every journey. An archaeological tool can reveal technique and activity; it cannot tell us exactly what its maker was thinking. Good reconstruction comes from making these different kinds of evidence talk to one another without pretending they are the same thing.

The Wrong Question: Where Did the First Human Live?

Continue

The wider lesson of **The Wrong Question: Where Did the First Human Live?** is that human history is a network of local stories. Geography matters because people have always responded to water, food, temperature, predators, disease, coastlines and terrain. Technology matters because a route that was impossible for one population may have been manageable for another. Social learning matters because knowledge can travel farther and faster than bodies. And chance matters: a small group can become the ancestor of a much larger later population without being the only people alive at the time. This is why the phrase “the movement” should be used carefully. There were movements inside Africa, movements between Africa and Eurasia, movements along coasts and rivers, and later movements between islands and continents. Some became major population expansions; others became dead ends in the surviving genetic record.

For readers, the practical test is simple: separate the observation from the story built around it. **Observation:** a fossil, tool assemblage, genetic lineage, environmental layer or archaeological settlement occurs at a particular time and place. **Interpretation:** researchers connect those observations into a model of population history. **Speculation:** a proposed route, identity, motive or cultural meaning that the available evidence does not establish by itself. This volume keeps those levels visible. It also avoids treating modern racial labels as if they were ancient biological populations. Human groups changed, mixed and moved continuously, while the categories later imposed on them changed with politics and geography. The deeper story is therefore not a catalogue of separate human races. It is a history of populations repeatedly meeting, separating and changing.

What the volume can establish — and what it cannot

The broad picture is strong: Homo sapiens evolved in Africa; human populations moved repeatedly within and beyond Africa; later populations interacted with Neanderthals and Denisovan-related groups; and humans eventually occupied every continent. The details are much less simple.

There is no scientifically defensible need for a single “first human” who suddenly appeared, a single pure ancestral population, or a fixed set of ancient biological races corresponding to modern social categories. The evidence instead points toward populations that changed over time and interacted across geography.

Likewise, “race” and “ancestry” should not be used as synonyms. Modern racial classifications are social systems whose meanings and boundaries changed historically. Genetic ancestry concerns inherited relationships among populations and individuals. The two can intersect in people's lives, but they answer different questions.

Editorial standard: This volume distinguishes scientific consensus, active research questions, and illustrative hypotheses. It does not treat a dramatic possibility as a fact merely because the possibility makes a better story.

Frequently asked questions

Did humans evolve from modern apes?
No. Humans and living great apes share common ancestors; modern chimpanzees and gorillas are not our direct ancestors.
Where did Homo sapiens originate?
Current evidence places the origin of Homo sapiens in Africa, with fossils and genetics indicating a continent-wide, structured history rather than a single village or point.
Was there one migration out of Africa?
Evidence supports multiple dispersals. A major expansion occurred within the last 60,000 years, while earlier movements outside Africa are also documented.
Is race a biological division of humanity?
Modern biological anthropology and genomics do not support socially defined races as discrete biological populations. Human variation is real, but it is distributed through overlapping and changing population histories.
What is the Gompht?
In this volume it is a nickname for Gomphotherium, an extinct proboscidean genus. Many gomphotheres had four tusks; it was not a human ancestor.
Did humans meet Gomphotherium?
That depends on the species and chronology being discussed. The volume treats possible overlap cautiously rather than asserting a universal human–Gomphotherium encounter.
What was Beringia?
A large Ice Age region connecting northeastern Asia and northwestern North America when sea level was much lower. It was more than a narrow 'bridge.'
How did humans reach Sahul?
Lower sea levels joined Australia and New Guinea into Sahul, but people still had to cross water gaps through Wallacea.
Why are maps of migration sometimes different?
Different maps summarize different datasets, dates, routes and assumptions. Some are based on mitochondrial lineages, others on archaeology, climate models or genome-wide evidence.
What remains unknown?
Many things: exact population sizes, the identities of unsampled African groups, precise routes for particular dispersals, the timing of some contacts, and the meanings of behaviors that leave no direct record.
